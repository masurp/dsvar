How this course works

Every chart makes a claim, and someone chose which one. This course teaches you to read that claim in other people’s charts, and to make your own charts in R so that the claim they make is the one your data supports.

New here? Start with Before the first work group. How you are graded is on Assessment, what you may use AI for on AI use, and every date and room on Weeks.

WHAT YOU WILL LEARN

01

Reading a chart as a claim

Say what a chart claims, and whether its data supports that claim. Run a critique in four steps (describe, analyse, interpret, judge) on any published visualisation.

02

The grammar of graphics

Describe any chart as eight settings, from data and mapping to theme, and build it in R with ggplot2, one setting at a time.

03

The right form for the data

Choose a chart by the kind of variables you have: comparisons and rankings, distributions, relationships, change over time, maps, networks and text.

04

Showing uncertainty

Show what a chart cannot tell you: an n, a range, an interval, the whole distribution, or the spread of defensible analyses rather than one of them.

05

Honest and misleading choices

Name the settings that change a chart's story (baseline, order, scale, denominator, time window) and build the misleading version on purpose, so you recognise it in other people's work.

06

A data story of your own

Choose a dataset, annotate and colour your charts for a reader, and present the result as a data story or an explanatory blog post, with code that runs on someone else's machine.

HOW A WEEK WORKS

Seven weeks of teaching, each adding one kind of variable to what you can draw, and an eighth with no sessions, for the take-home assignment. Weeks 2 to 7 all have the same rhythm.

Tuesday · lecture

Understanding visualizations

Each lecture opens with a critique of charts from the previous Thursday, without names, and then works through one kind of comparison: what it can show, the settings that change its story, and published examples that got it right or wrong.

Thursday · work group

Building visualizations in R

Meet a dataset the lecture did not use, draw the chart on paper, build it in R, and then twist it into a misleading version. The pair are photographed (screenshot) at the end for the next Tuesday. Week 1 is different: it introduces R and the grammar of graphics, and nothing is handed in.

Sunday · portfolio entry

Finish the visualization pair at home

At times, you may not finish part II of the workgroups. You just finish the honest chart and its misleading twin from Thursday and submit it with code that runs. Six in total, one each Sunday of weeks 2 to 7.

WHAT WE EXPECT FROM YOU

  • Come to every lecture and every work group. Attendance is mandatory, although it is not recorded.
  • Bring your own laptop to every work group, with R, RStudio, the tidyverse and Quarto installed and the setup check passed.
  • Run everything yourself. The code on these pages is shown, not run: what it prints is for you to find out.
  • Draw before you code. The sketch on paper is what you are building.
  • Say something. The lectures open with a critique of your charts, and it only works if the room talks.
  • Say how you used AI. The rules are on the AI use page.

THE TOOL

The course uses R. Everything you produce is made with ggplot2, and so is everything you are shown: every chart on these pages and in every lecture deck is R code you could have written, styled with the same theme_dsvar() you will be given. The exceptions are a handful of historical originals in the gallery, such as Minard, Nightingale and Snow, where the original itself is the point.
