AI use · 2026

Using AI in this course

AI tools are everywhere, and we cannot realistically make you work without them. What we can do is decide where they help your learning, and hold you accountable for what you hand in. The rule is the same every week: no AI in Part I of a work group, where you learn the code; AI for language only in the two critique pieces; and AI with a statement in Part II, the portfolio and the take-home. Wherever AI is allowed, you say how you used it, can account for every chart you hand in, and can own the claim it makes. This page explains where AI fits, the four kinds of use, the statement you write about them, and how AI was used to make this course; the Assessment page explains how the work is graded.

WHERE AI FITS

An AI tool can write a ggplot call in seconds. You can only tell whether that call draws the right chart if you can read and write one yourself, and if you know which claim the data supports and which choice in the chart does the work. That is what the first part of every work group teaches, so it is done without AI. Once you can write the code, a tool can write it faster, and you can judge what it gives you.

No AI

Part I, and the sketch

Part I of each work group is where you learn the code: you run every chunk and write the two Your turn exercises yourself. No AI, and no code completion such as GitHub Copilot: switch it off. Week 1 works the same way, and so does the sketch on paper that starts Part II.

Language only

The two critique pieces

A critique is your own reading of someone else’s chart. AI may improve your language (grammar, spelling, translation) but may not add content: no descriptions, arguments or verdicts. The piece still ends with an AI statement that says what it changed.

AI allowed, with a statement

Part II, portfolio, take-home

From the Part II build on, you may use AI as an assistant, a thinking partner or an instrument. Each piece ends with an AI statement. Never as a surrogate: you may not hand in work a tool did that you cannot defend without its output in front of you.

The reference sheet on every work group page runs on different data from the week’s file, on purpose: pasting it, or a tool’s version of it, draws a chart of the wrong thing.

FOUR KINDS OF USE

We sort AI use by what the tool did to the work, and so by what you have to be able to account for.

Kind What it means What we ask of you
Assistant Syntax, error messages, help searching, and writing or fixing the code for a chart you have already designed. Editing, grammar and translation of your text. The claim, the design and the reasoning are yours; the tool touched how they are written. Which tool, for what, and what you did with its output: what you kept, what you changed, what you rejected. And how you checked that the chart shows what the data says.
Thinking partner The model works on your own sketch, chart or draft. You ask it what a reader might misread, which choice in your twin does the work, or what your critique has missed. The ideas you test are already yours. Say what you discussed with it and what came out of it. You must be able to defend every claim without the output in front of you, and the text must be in your own voice.
Instrument The model's output becomes part of your data: it sorts or labels rows, reads numbers off a document or an image, or produces a table you then chart. A statement written like a methods section: which model, what you asked it, and how you checked its output against the source or your own judgement.
Surrogate The model produces the work (the chart, the claim, the critique) and you put your name on it. That includes a chart the tool drew itself, in its own window, rather than the R code in your file. Not allowed. It falls under the VU fraud and plagiarism regulation, and no AI statement makes it acceptable.

The rule from the Info page holds here too: every chart you hand in is made with ggplot2, by the code in your file, and that code has to run on someone else’s machine.

THE AI STATEMENT

WHAT IT HAS TO SAY

Every piece you hand in ends with a section headed AI use: each portfolio entry (the entry file in each week’s folder already has it), both critique pieces and the take-home. Write one short block for each kind of use, and say which parts you did without AI. For each use, answer four questions:

  1. Which tool, and when (e.g. ChatGPT, 12 November; Claude; GitHub Copilot in RStudio)?
  2. What for: which chart or step, and what you asked it?
  3. What you did with it: what you kept, what you changed, and at least one thing you rejected, with the reason.
  4. How you checked it: how you know the chart shows what the data says, not only that the code runs. For example, you compared one plotted value with the table, or the n in your caption with nrow().

If you used no AI at all, write No AI used. That is a complete statement. If you used it a lot, paste your most important prompts at the end of the file, or add a share link to the conversation.

The standard behind all of it: you must be able to explain and defend every choice in your chart (the claim, the encoding, the scale, the twin) without the output in front of you. If you cannot, the kind of use was Surrogate, whatever the statement says.

Example · light use

Only as an assistant

Assistant. ChatGPT (5 Nov), for one error in the build: “Cannot use + with a single argument”. It explained that I had started a line with + instead of ending the line before with it. I moved the + myself.

Not used for: the claim, the sketch, the twin and the sentence on which choice did the work.

Example · medium use

Assistant and thinking partner

Assistant. Claude (19 Nov), to write the geom_text_repel() call for the labels. I kept its code and rejected its suggestion to label every point: the claim is about four countries, so only those carry a name. I checked those four against the table.

Thinking partner. After building my twin, I asked which choice a reader would notice first. It said the log scale; I think the window does more of the work, and my sentence says why.

Not used for: the claim and the sketch. Prompts at the end of the file.

Example · entry 6, your own data

When the model touches the data

Instrument. ChatGPT (10 Dec), to copy a 30-row table out of the PDF report my data comes from. I checked every row against the PDF; two were shifted by a line, and I fixed them by hand. The caption names the report, not the tool, as the source.

Assistant. ChatGPT, to turn my sketch into a first ggplot() call. It mapped colour to region; I removed that, because the claim compares years, not regions.

Not used for: the claim, the twin and the text.

NOT ENOUGH

AI use: ChatGPT helped with the code and the text.

Which code? Which chart? What did you keep, what did you change, what did you reject, and how did you check it? A statement like this counts as missing.

IF IT GOES WRONG

What happens
No statement, or one like the example above It counts as missing.
Work a tool did, handed in as yours Surrogate use falls under the VU fraud and plagiarism regulation, with or without a statement.

OUR OWN AI STATEMENT

Because we ask you to write an AI statement, we lead by example: here is how I used AI to make this course, in the same format. It covers what I prepared as coordinator: this website, the slides, the work groups and the gallery. The standard is the same: I can account for every page, every chart and every number in them, and the errors in them are mine (tell us on Canvas when you find one).

AI statement · the coordinator

Assistant. Claude Code (Anthropic, Claude Opus models) was used to build this website, to write parts of the R code behind the slides and the gallery rebuilds, and to check all R code in the slides and the work groups for errors and consistency. I reviewed everything before it went online. I could check the code because I wrote this kind of code for years without AI, which is why Part I of every work group is done without it.

Claude also helped drafting slides and pages from my outlines and notes and from the materials of earlier editions of this course. I closely reviewed and heavily edited everything it produced. It also checked that dates, rules and numbers agree across the site and the slides.

Not used for: grading or feedback on your work.
