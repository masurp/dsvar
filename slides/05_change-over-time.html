Wheat and wages over 250 years (1821)

What does this chart show?

Chart, Shewing at One View the Price of the Quarter of Wheat, & Wages of Labour by the Week, from the Year 1565 to 1821, Chart No. 1 in W. Playfair, A Letter on Our Agricultural Distresses (London, 1821). Public domain; scan via Wikimedia Commons.

Who drew it, and what he wanted to show

  • William Playfair, a Scottish engineer, drew the first line chart and the first bar chart in 1786. This one is from a pamphlet he sent to Parliament in 1821, when English farmers were in trouble
  • His claim: a working man had never been better off, because his wage bought more wheat than ever
  • He collected the numbers from price records and wage estimates going back to 1565
  • How to read it: each dark bar is the price of a quarter of wheat, about 220 kg, averaged over five years. The red line is the weekly wage of a skilled workman. Both are in shillings, on the same scale, with the monarchs along the top

The data: 53 rows, one every five years

Six of the 53 rows

year wheat wages
1565 41 5.00
1570 45 5.05
1575 42 5.08
1710 44 11.00
1805 81 29.50
1810 99 30.00
  • One row is five years: the year it starts, the price of a quarter of wheat and a week’s wage, both in shillings
  • Nobody has Playfair’s own table. Statisticians measured these numbers off his chart, and they ship with R’s HistData package
  • The wage series stops in 1810, so the last three rows have a price and no wage
  • Wheat was dearest in 1810: 99 shillings a quarter

Friendly, M. (2021). HistData: Data sets from the history of statistics and data visualization (the Wheat dataset). Source: Playfair (1821), public domain.

Rebuilt in R

ggplot(wheat, aes(year)) +
  geom_col(aes(y = wheat), fill = "grey75",
           width = 4) +
  geom_line(aes(y = wages),
            colour = dsvar_accent(),
            linewidth = 1.2) +
  labs(x = NULL, y = "Shillings",
       title = "Wheat jumps, wages creep")
  • Two columns, two geoms, one y axis: both are shillings
  • The bars are what a quarter of wheat cost, the line is what a week of work paid. The eye reads the space between them
  • What would that space be, in words? Shillings per quarter minus shillings per week is not a quantity

One change: divide one by the other

wheat |>
  mutate(weeks = wheat / wages) |>
  ggplot(aes(year, weeks)) +
  geom_line(colour = dsvar_accent(),
            linewidth = 1.2) +
  expand_limits(y = 0) +
  labs(x = NULL, y = "Weeks of wages",
       title = paste("A quarter of wheat cost",
                     "fewer weeks of work"))
  • Playfair’s claim is about how the wage stands against the price, so draw that: the price in weeks of wages
  • Before 1600 a quarter cost 9.1 weeks of a mechanic’s wage. After 1800 it cost 2.9
  • He drew this himself two pages later, as Chart No. 3. Part VIII comes back to two series on one axis

The warming stripes (2018)

What does this chart show?

“#ShowYourStripes”, global average 1850–2025, by Ed Hawkins, National Centre for Atmospheric Science, University of Reading. showyourstripes.info, CC BY 4.0. Data: Met Office HadCRUT5.

Who made it, and how to read it

  • Ed Hawkins, a climate scientist at the University of Reading, published the first stripes in 2018. He wanted a picture of warming that needs no axis and no explanation, for people who never read a chart
  • They have since been printed on scarves, trams, football shirts and the cover of The Economist
  • How to read it: each stripe is one year, 1850 on the left and 2025 on the right. Blue years were cooler than a reference average, red years warmer, and a deeper colour is further from it
  • You can see that it got warmer and roughly when. You cannot read how much

The data: one number for each year

Six of the 176 rows

year anomaly lo hi
1850 -0.42 -0.59 -0.25
1851 -0.23 -0.41 -0.05
1961 -0.02 -0.07 0.03
1962 -0.06 -0.12 -0.01
2024 1.18 1.14 1.22
2025 1.19 1.05 1.33
  • One row is one year: the temperature of the whole planet’s surface, as one number
  • It is built from thousands of weather stations on land and from ships and buoys at sea, combined by the Met Office and the University of East Anglia
  • anomaly is not a temperature. It is the difference from the average of 1961–1990, in °C. Part IV builds one of these from scratch
  • lo and hi are the ends of a 95% interval: the early years rest on far fewer thermometers

Deaths since the tenth death (2020)

You read this in week 1. What is on its x axis?

“Coronavirus deaths in Italy, Spain and the US are increasing more rapidly than they did in China”. John Burn-Murdoch, Financial Times, data to 26 March 2020. © The Financial Times Limited; shown at reduced size for criticism and review.

Who made it, and how to read it

  • John Burn-Murdoch, a data journalist at the Financial Times, updated this chart every day from March 2020. The numbers were each country’s reported deaths, collected by health ministries and the WHO
  • How to read it: each line is one country, and the height is its deaths so far, a running total
  • The x axis is not the calendar. Day 0 is the day that country recorded its tenth death, so outbreaks that began weeks apart start at the same point
  • The y axis is logarithmic, last week’s setting: an equal step multiplies by the same factor, so a straight line is a constant doubling time

Rebuilt in R

aligned <- covid |>
  filter(total_deaths >= 10) |>
  group_by(country) |>
  mutate(day = as.numeric(date - min(date))) |>
  filter(day <= 60)

ggplot(aligned, aes(day, total_deaths,
                    group = country)) +
  geom_line(colour = "grey60") +
  geom_line(data = filter(aligned,
                          country == "Italy"),
            colour = dsvar_accent(),
            linewidth = 1.2) +
  scale_y_log10(labels = scales::comma) +
  labs(x = "Days since the 10th death",
       y = "Deaths so far (log scale)",
       title = "Italy: 6,820 deaths in four weeks")
  • covid has one row per country and day, 13 countries, from Our World in Data
  • A date minus a date is a number of days. Subtracting each country’s first day with ten deaths starts every line at 0

Italy and South Korea both recorded their tenth death on 26 February 2020. Four weeks later Italy had 6,820 deaths and South Korea 126: the flat grey line.

One change: back to the calendar

  • One word changed in aes(): date for day
  • The countries reached their tenth death over 31 days, so the lines now start in different places and their slopes are hard to compare

Both charts are true. The calendar answers who was hit first. Days since the tenth death answers whose outbreak grew faster, and the axis label says which clock is running.

Since when? Compared to what?

A price against a wage, a year against a normal, a country against its own tenth death. Each chart of time is read against something, and somebody chose it.

Week 4 added principle 8 and four settings under it: how points pile up, which line is drawn, what an equal step means, and the third variable. This week adds principle 9, for one variable measured again and again.

What a series is made of

  • Last week both variables were measured once per person. This week one thing is measured again and again, and one row is one moment
  • The rows have an order, and neighbours resemble each other. That is why a line may join them, which last week’s points did not allow
  • A real series is several of these added up. Most of today is about telling them apart: what is the season, what is the trend, and what is left
  • These four panels are drawn for this slide, not measured

What El Niño is

  • In most years, winds push the warm surface water of the Pacific west, towards Indonesia. Cold water rises off Peru
  • Every few years the winds weaken, the warm water spreads back east, and the middle of the ocean warms for about a year. Peruvian fishermen named it El Niño, the Christ child, because it peaks around Christmas
  • It moves rain around the world: floods in Peru, drought in Australia and southern Africa. The cold opposite is La Niña
  • To know whether one is under way, NOAA, the US weather agency, watches the water in one box

The dataset: 2,350 weeks of one patch of ocean

Six of the 2,350 rows

week temp
1981-09-02 26.5
1981-09-09 26.5
1981-09-16 26.5
2026-08-26 29.4
2026-09-02 29.4
2026-09-09 29.6
  • One row is one week: the date of its Wednesday, and the average temperature of the sea surface in the box, in °C
  • The temperature is measured by satellites, by ships and by buoys anchored along the equator, and NOAA blends them into one number a week
  • It runs from 2 September 1981 to 9 September 2026. The warmest week was 29.8 °C, on 18 November 2015, and the coldest 24.1 °C
  • There is no sample here and no interval: the same place, measured every week. What varies is time

A date is a count of days

  • R stores a date as a number and prints it as year-month-day. So dates sort, subtract, and sit on an axis at the right distance from each other
  • A date written like 2026-09-09 is recognised when the dataset is read. The week column arrived as a date
raw <- c("02SEP1981", "09SEP1981", "06JAN1982")

  • Anything else arrives as text. This is how NOAA writes the week in its own file. Text sorts by alphabet, so January 1982 comes first, and a plot would put the weeks in that order
  • dmy() reads day, month, year and returns dates. ym() reads a year and a month, as the stripes’ monthly record writes them, and fills in the first of the month

II · Building a line chart with ggplot

Start with points

ggplot(nino, aes(week, temp)) +
  geom_point()
  • Time goes on the x axis, the measurement on the y axis. One point per week
  • Because week is a date, ggplot spaces the points by the calendar and writes years under them
  • The points form a band between 24 and 30 °C. Which week follows which is lost

Join them

ggplot(nino, aes(week, temp)) +
  geom_line()
  • geom_line() joins each week to the next one. A line says: these belong together, in this order
  • Now two movements show. A wave once a year, about a degree high: the season
  • And every few years a surge to 29 °C or more, or a drop to 25: 1982–83, 1997–98, 2015–16

Label the time axis

ggplot(nino, aes(week, temp)) +
  geom_line() +
  scale_x_date(
    breaks = seq(as.Date("1985-01-01"),
                 as.Date("2025-01-01"),
                 by = "5 years"),
    date_labels = "%Y")
  • scale_x_date() is the scale for a date axis. breaks takes the dates to label, and seq() counts from one date to another in steps of five years
  • date_labels says how to write them: %Y is the year, %b the month’s short name, %d the day. "%b %Y" gives Sep 2026
  • A label every five years is enough to find 1998 and 2016

Add the claim

ggplot(nino, aes(week, temp)) +
  geom_line() +
  scale_x_date(
    breaks = seq(as.Date("1985-01-01"),
                 as.Date("2025-01-01"),
                 by = "5 years"),
    date_labels = "%Y") +
  labs(
    title = "Every few years the Pacific runs hot",
    subtitle = "Sea surface, Niño 3.4 region",
    x = NULL, y = "°C",
    caption = paste0(
      "n = 2,350 weeks, Sep 1981 to Sep 2026 · ",
      "NOAA\n",
      "The yearly wave is the season, ",
      "not El Niño"))
  • The title says the finding. An axis of years needs no title, so x = NULL
  • The caption says how many weeks, from when to when, and what the chart cannot separate yet: season and El Niño are drawn in one line

III · The same weeks, arranged four ways

The newest week is 29.6 °C. Is that warm?

Along the calendar

  • One mark: the red point is the week of 9 September 2026, at 29.6 °C
  • Against the whole record it is high: only 1 of 2,350 weeks were warmer
  • But it is September, and Septembers are usually cool here. To judge it, compare it with other Septembers
  • On this chart those are 45 places, a year apart. The eye cannot line them up

Put the years on top of each other

ggplot(nino, aes(yday, temp, group = year)) +
  geom_line(colour = "grey70") +
  geom_line(data = filter(nino, year == 2026),
            colour = dsvar_accent(),
            linewidth = 1.2) +
  labs(x = "Day of the year", y = "°C")
  • The x axis is now yday, the day within the year. group = year draws one line per year
  • Every September sits above the same place on the axis. This year, in green, runs far above all the others at this time of year
  • The grey lines show the season: warmest in April and May, coolest around the new year

The New York Times published this form as its El Niño tracker on 24 August 2026, with daily values. Ours is rebuilt from NOAA’s weekly dataset.

Or lay them side by side

ggplot(nino, aes(yday, year, fill = temp)) +
  geom_tile(width = 7) +
  scale_fill_viridis_c(option = "magma") +
  scale_y_reverse() +
  labs(x = "Day of the year", y = NULL,
       fill = "°C")
  • A heat map: every week is a small tile, its row is the year, and the temperature is the colour
  • Read down a column for the same week in 45 years. Read along a row for one year
  • The bright rows are the El Niño years. Colour is a weaker channel than position (week 1), so which years stand out is easy and by how much is not

Or bend the year into a circle

ggplot(nino, aes(yday, temp, group = year)) +
  geom_line(colour = "grey70") +
  geom_line(data = filter(nino, year == 2026),
            colour = dsvar_accent(),
            linewidth = 1.2) +
  coord_polar() +
  labs(x = "Day of the year", y = "°C")
  • One line added to the chart before last. coord_polar() wraps the x axis around a centre, so December meets January
  • It shows that the year is a loop, and warm years sit further out
  • Reading a value is harder: the temperature is now a distance from the centre. Ed Hawkins’s climate spiral is this form, on the stripes’ data

Principle 3, for time

Put the moments you want compared next to each other
  • Along the calendar puts each week next to the week before. It answers: what happened, in which order?
  • Years on top of each other puts each week next to the same week of other years. It answers: is this unusual for the season?
  • Week 1’s principle 3 put what matters in the strongest channel. For a series, that also decides which times share a position
  • The stacked years still leave one thing to the eye: how far the green line is above the usual. Part IV turns that distance into a number

IV · What counts as normal

29.6 °C in September. How far is that from a normal September?

The normal: an average for each month

normal <- nino |>
  filter(year >= 1991, year <= 2020) |>
  group_by(month) |>
  summarise(normal = mean(temp))
head(normal, 4)
# A tibble: 4 × 2
  month normal
  <ord>  <dbl>
1 Jan     26.5
2 Feb     26.7
3 Mar     27.2
4 Apr     27.8
  • Take thirty years, 1991 to 2020. Average all their Januaries, all their Februaries, and so on: twelve numbers
  • That is the normal, or baseline: what the water usually is at that time of year
  • The black dots are those twelve numbers, drawn over the years they come from

Subtract it: the anomaly

nino <- nino |>
  left_join(normal, by = "month") |>
  mutate(anomaly = temp - normal)
  • left_join() gives every week the normal of its month. Then one subtraction
  • An anomaly is the difference from normal for that time of year. It is still in °C, but 0 now means as usual
  • The stripes’ anomaly column was made the same way, with 1961–1990 as the thirty years

Three of the 2,350 rows

week month temp normal anomaly
1988-11-30 Nov 24.1 26.7 -2.6
2013-06-05 Jun 27.3 27.7 -0.4
2026-09-09 Sep 29.6 26.7 2.9
  • 9 September 2026: 29.6 − 26.7 = +2.9 °C. Nearly three degrees warmer than a normal September
  • The 1988 week is 2.6 °C below its normal: a La Niña
  • NOAA publishes its own anomaly for every week. Ours agrees with it at r = 0.99

The anomaly, along the calendar

ggplot(nino, aes(week, anomaly)) +
  annotate("rect", xmin = min(nino$week),
           xmax = max(nino$week),
           ymin = -.5, ymax = .5,
           alpha = .15) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = 0,
             colour = "grey50",
             linetype = "dashed") +
  geom_line() +
  labs(x = NULL,
       y = "°C above or below normal")
  • The yearly wave is gone. What is left is how unusual each week was
  • The grey band is ±0.5 °C. NOAA calls it an El Niño when the water stays above the band for several months
  • Five strong ones in 45 years, and the last is under way

Move the normal, and the same water changes

  • Until 2021 NOAA used 1981–2010 as its thirty years. Then it moved to 1991–2020
  • The ocean had warmed in between, so the new normal is 0.11 °C higher on average
  • A tenth of a degree is little. But El Niño is defined by a threshold: with the older normal, 679 of these weeks are at +0.5 °C or more. With the newer one, 591
  • The water did not change. The stripes turned from red to blue the same way

Setting 1 · What counts as normal

Say what zero stands for: which years, and whose choice
  • Ask of any chart with time on it: above or below what? Which period is the normal, and who picked it?
  • A normal removes what everyone already knows, here the season, so that what is left is the news. It also decides what looks alarming and what looks ordinary
  • “Compared to 2010 = 100” and “since 2000” are normals too. Part IX meets one on a scale that has no zero
  • The stripes’ record uses 1961–1990, NOAA’s uses 1991–2020, and the Paris target of 1.5 °C counts from 1850–1900

V · Where it starts and ends

A series has a first and a last value on the chart. Somebody picked both.

A chart that falls

nino |>
  filter(week >= "2015-11-18",
         week <= "2018-01-31") |>
  ggplot(aes(week, temp)) +
  geom_line() +
  geom_smooth(method = "lm") +
  labs(x = NULL, y = "°C",
       title = "The Pacific has cooled by 3.9 °C")
  • 116 weeks. The first is 29.8 °C and the last 25.9 °C: the title is true
  • The fitted line falls by 1.2 °C a year, and its band is narrow
  • Nothing is wrong with the numbers or the model. Look at the two dates in filter()

The same weeks, inside the record

  • The window starts on the warmest week in the record, the peak of the 2015–16 El Niño, and ends in a La Niña
  • In a series that swings, a window from a peak to a trough falls. Over all 2,350 weeks the fitted line rises by 0.06 °C a decade
  • Drawn inside the record, the same weeks make the same narrow claim, and the reader can see where they came from

The same move, on the planet

  • The stripes’ record again, by month: 84 months from January 2016 to December 2022
  • The fitted line falls by 0.16 °C a decade. The window opens on the 2016 El Niño and closes on three La Niña years
  • Since 1970 the same record rises by 0.20 °C a decade
  • “Warming stopped in 1998” was made this way, from the 1997–98 El Niño. Its window, 1998 to 2012, still rises by 0.12 °C a decade

Setting 2 · Where it starts and ends

Say why the chart starts where it starts
  • Ask of any chart with time on it: why these years? Would the claim hold if it began three years earlier?
  • A good window has a reason outside the chart: a law changed, a survey began, the claim is about that decade. A window that starts on the highest point has none
  • The FT chart chose its start by a rule and printed the rule on the axis: days since the tenth death
  • If the claim is about a short stretch, draw the stretch inside the whole record

VI · How much is smoothed away

One week is weather. To see the El Niño, average over more than one.

A rolling mean: each week with its neighbours

nino <- nino |>
  mutate(smooth = zoo::rollmean(anomaly, 13,
                                fill = NA))
nino |>
  select(week, anomaly, smooth) |>
  slice(5:10)
# A tibble: 6 × 3
  week       anomaly smooth
  <date>       <dbl>  <dbl>
1 1981-09-30 -0.487  NA    
2 1981-10-07 -0.234  NA    
3 1981-10-14 -0.334  -0.416
4 1981-10-21 -0.0338 -0.408
5 1981-10-28 -0.434  -0.424
6 1981-11-04 -1.04   -0.477
  • A rolling mean replaces each value by the average of itself and its neighbours. Here 13 weeks, about three months: the week, six before and six after
  • rollmean() is from the zoo package. fill = NA keeps one row per week
  • The first six weeks have no six weeks before them, so they stay empty. So do the last six, and those are the newest ones
  • NOAA’s official El Niño index is a three-month rolling mean of the anomaly

Draw it, then widen it

nino |>
  mutate(smooth = zoo::rollmean(anomaly, 13,
                                fill = NA)) |>
  ggplot(aes(week, anomaly)) +
  geom_line(colour = "grey75") +
  geom_line(aes(y = smooth),
            colour = dsvar_accent(),
            linewidth = 1)
nino |>
  mutate(smooth = zoo::rollmean(anomaly, 52,
                                fill = NA)) |>
  ggplot(aes(week, anomaly)) +
  geom_line(colour = "grey75") +
  geom_line(aes(y = smooth),
            colour = dsvar_accent(),
            linewidth = 1)
nino |>
  mutate(smooth = zoo::rollmean(anomaly, 260,
                                fill = NA)) |>
  ggplot(aes(week, anomaly)) +
  geom_line(colour = "grey75") +
  geom_line(aes(y = smooth),
            colour = dsvar_accent(),
            linewidth = 1)
  • 13 weeks: the weekly jitter is gone, every El Niño and La Niña is still there. The data stays underneath, in grey
  • 52 weeks: each event is lower and wider. A year’s average mixes its peak with the months around it
  • 260 weeks, five years: no event is left, and the line stops in March 2024. This year’s El Niño is not on it

Loess: the same idea, another knob

ggplot(nino, aes(week, anomaly)) +
  geom_line(colour = "grey75") +
  geom_smooth(method = "loess",
              span = .05, se = FALSE,
              colour = dsvar_accent())
ggplot(nino, aes(week, anomaly)) +
  geom_line(colour = "grey75") +
  geom_smooth(method = "loess",
              span = .75, se = FALSE,
              colour = dsvar_accent())
  • Last week’s loess fits a small straight line around every point, using the nearest part of the data. span is how large that part is, as a share: .05 is the nearest 5% of the weeks
  • span = .75 is ggplot’s default. On this series it removes everything the chart was about
  • A rolling mean counts its width in weeks, loess in a share of the data. Either way the caption has to give the number

Setting 3 · How much is smoothed away

Smooth to the length of what you claim, and keep the data under the line
  • Ask of any smooth line: averaged over how long? What shorter than that can it no longer show?
  • No width is the correct one. Three months fits a claim about El Niño, thirty years a claim about climate. Week 3’s bins and bandwidths worked the same way
  • A centred average cannot reach the newest values, and the newest values are usually what the argument is about
  • The FT later drew daily deaths as a seven-day average, because fewer deaths are registered at weekends

VII · How the box is shaped

One line, three boxes

  • The stripes’ record, 176 yearly values, drawn three times by the same code. Only the height of the figure differs
  • How steep a line looks depends on the box. People judge slopes best when the lines lie at around 45 degrees (Cleveland et al., 1988)
  • So the width and height you give a figure belong to the chart as much as its scales do

Cleveland, W. S., McGill, M. E., & McGill, R. (1988). The shape parameter of a two-variable graph. Journal of the American Statistical Association, 83(402), 289–300.

Setting 4 · How the box is shaped

Choose the width and height so the slopes can be read
  • Ask of any line chart: would this look as dramatic, or as calm, in a box half as tall?
  • Aim for slopes near 45 degrees in the part the claim is about. A long series usually wants a wide box
  • Two charts that are meant to be compared need the same box, and the same y axis

Two series, one time axis

Readers read the gap and the crossing. Draw them only if they mean something
  • Same quantity, same unit: one axis is right, and the gap is a real difference. Ten stations’ temperatures, two countries’ death counts
  • A relation between two quantities: divide, as Playfair did in his Chart No. 3, or put one against the other in a scatterplot
  • Otherwise: two panels with a shared time axis
  • A second y axis is safe for one thing only: the same quantity in two units, °C on the left and °F on the right

IX · Twenty years of headlines

The same four questions, on data from the social sciences.

Are headlines getting more negative? (2022)

  • David Rozado and two colleagues collected 23 million headlines from 47 American news outlets, 2000 to 2019
  • A language model scored each headline from −1, negative, to +1, positive. There is no neutral score
  • How to read it: left to right is the year. The line is the average score of that year’s headlines, and the band is its 95% interval
  • The annotation says the average fell by 314%

Fig 1 from Rozado, D., Hughes, R., & Halberstadt, J. (2022). Longitudinal analysis of sentiment and emotion in news media headlines. PLOS ONE, 17(10), e0276367. CC BY 4.0.

The four questions, asked

  • Normal. −314% compares 2019 with 2000. But 0 on this scale is not no sentiment, so a percentage of it means nothing. In points: -0.24, on a scale from −1 to +1
  • Start and end. Outlets joined every year, so the average of 2019 is not the average of the same outlets as in 2000. The 18 present throughout fall by 0.16
  • Smoothed. Each point is a year’s average of outlet averages. A small outlet counts as much as a large one
  • Box. The original’s y axis shows about a sixth of the scale from −1 to +1, so the fall fills the panel

A chart made to persuade without regard for whether it informs is what Correll (2019) calls bullshit. Your portfolio twin should need only one setting.
