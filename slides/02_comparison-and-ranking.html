A famous bar chart from 1900

What does this chart show?

Illiteracy of the American Negroes compared with that of other nations. W. E. B. Du Bois and students of Atlanta University, Exposition Universelle, Paris, 1900. Library of Congress, LCCN 2014645352. Public domain; scan via Wikimedia Commons.

Who drew it, and what it answered

  • W. E. B. Du Bois, a sociologist and the first Black American to earn a doctorate at Harvard. With his students at Atlanta University he drew about sixty charts by hand for the 1900 World’s Fair in Paris
  • The charts answered a claim. In 1896 the American Economic Association had published Frederick Hoffman’s Race Traits and Tendencies of the American Negro, which used census figures to argue that Black Americans had declined since slavery ended
  • Under slavery, teaching an enslaved person to read had been a crime in most slave states. Thirty-five years later, this plate asks how far Black Americans had come
  • How to read it: each bar is one population, and its length is the share who could not read or write. The red bar is Black Americans, the green bars are nations, named in French. “Negroes” is the plate’s word, the usual one in 1900

  • One number comes from outside: Black Americans’ 56.8 per cent in the 1890 census. Set the red bar to it, and the other nine follow

Two lines changed who is in the chart, and the 56.8 per cent did not move. Both charts are true. Du Bois asked whether Black Americans could advance, so he set them beside nations. The gap with white Americans had been imposed by law.

The gap, from 1870 to 1930

The plate shows one year of a census taken every ten years. In 1870, five years after emancipation, four in five Black Americans could not read or write; by 1930, one in six, in schools that were segregated and short of money. Hoffman had claimed decline, and the series shows the opposite.

A league table, every three years (2023)

  • PISA, run by the OECD, tests a sample of 15-year-olds every three years. In 2022: mathematics, in 81 education systems
  • The scores sit on a scale set so that OECD countries averaged 500 when it began. In 2022 the OECD average was 472
  • Sorted by score. The place is never printed, and it is what everyone reads: the Netherlands is 10th
  • League tables move policy. Germany’s results in the first round, published in 2001, became the “PISA shock” and set off years of school reform

Table M1, Highlights of U.S. PISA 2022 Results Web Report (NCES 2023-115 and 2024-103). National Center for Education Statistics. Data: OECD, PISA 2022. Public domain.

The United States, 34th of 81

  • This is the US government’s table, so every row is compared with the United States
  • ◆ means not measurably different from the US. 12 systems get one, from France at 474 to Israel at 458
  • What the table cannot say is whether any of those 12 differ from each other
  • The ¹ after Vietnam: its sample covers only half to three quarters of the country’s 15-year-olds

A careful table: it marks which differences are real. Then it prints an exact order anyway.

One row per education system: its mean score, and the standard error of that mean, from NCES’s own data. These are the 21 systems within ten places of the United States. Dots, because a PISA score has no zero to grow a bar from (part IV).

One change: add each score’s uncertainty

pisa |>
  filter(between(place, 24, 44)) |>
  ggplot(aes(mean, fct_reorder(system, mean),
             colour = system == "United States")) +
  geom_errorbar(aes(xmin = mean - 1.96 * se,
                    xmax = mean + 1.96 * se), width = 0) +
  geom_point(size = 3) +
  scale_colour_manual(values = c("grey60", dsvar_accent())) +
  labs(x = "PISA mathematics score", y = NULL,
       title = "Most neighbours cannot be told apart") +
  theme(legend.position = "none")

Each line is the score’s 95 per cent interval. PISA tests a sample of students, so every mean could be a few points off. Where two lines overlap widely, the order between them is not settled.

Its place, and the places it could hold

On the table’s own tests the United States, printed 34th, could be anywhere from 26th to 38th. The Netherlands, printed 10th, could be anywhere from 9th to 18th. A rank is an estimate too (Goldstein & Spiegelhalter, 1996, on school and hospital league tables).

Twelve numbers around a circle (1877)

  • Georg Mayr, a Bavarian statistician, in Die Gesetzmässigkeit im Gesellschaftsleben (1877): a popular book on what statistics can say about society
  • Its first chapter teaches how to draw statistics. This page draws one series of twelve numbers, four ways
  • The column on the left is the data. No unit, no labels. Mayr’s own example suggests reading it as a year of months
  • a: each number is a spoke from the centre, the first at twelve o’clock, running clockwise. b: the same, measured in from the rim. c: the tips of a, joined up: the first radar chart. d: the same for b

Figur 4, Linien-Diagramme im Kreise, from G. Mayr, Die Gesetzmässigkeit im Gesellschaftsleben (Munich: Oldenbourg, 1877), p. 78. Public domain; scan via the Internet Archive.

His rule: only for things that come round

Zur Darstellung von Verhältnissen, welche thatsächlich einen Kreislauf darstellen, kann das in den Kreis gezeichnete Linien-Diagramm mit einiger Berechtigung angewendet werden.

For things that really are a cycle, a line diagram drawn in a circle can be used “with some justification”. His example: deaths by month, averaged over many years. Then January really is as close to December as to February.

On a straight line, December and January end up at opposite edges. In a circle they are neighbours again, and that is the one thing the circle adds. Mayr, p. 77; translation ours.

Rebuilt in R

mayr <- tibble(
  month = factor(month.abb, levels = month.abb),
  value = c(8, 9, 10, 8, 7, 6, 5, 4, 4, 5, 7, 7))

ggplot(mayr, aes(month, value)) +
  geom_col(width = .1) +
  coord_polar()

Without the last line this is a bar chart of months. coord_polar() bends its axis into a circle: last week’s coord slot. levels = month.abb puts the months in calendar order. Leave it out and they come out alphabetical, April first.

Compared to what?

Three charts, and not one number in the data set changed: Every comparison makes choices like these, but a reader never sees them.

“At the heart of quantitative reasoning is a single question: Compared to what?” Tufte, E. R. (1990). Envisioning Information. Graphics Press, p. 67.

Ranking and comparing

Ranking

  • One row per category: 51 states, 81 education systems, ten populations in 1900
  • Each bar or dot is one row of the data
  • The question: **where does each one stand among the others?

Comparing groups

  • Many rows per category: 1,711 people, shown six different feeds
  • Each dot is a summary of many rows, usually a mean, so it needs an interval and an n
  • The question: does the group make a difference?

Not every number has a zero

Kind You can say Today’s examples Treated as
Nominal same or different state, education system categorical
Ordinal more or less the feeds: 5, 25 or 90% personal posts categorical
Interval how much more PISA score, a 1-to-7 rating continuous
Ratio how many times income, the share of people who could not read continuous
  • Stanley Stevens’s four kinds of number, from 1946. Each one allows everything the row above allows, and one thing more
  • Only a ratio scale has a zero that means none: no income, no one
  • A bar’s length says how many times. Part IV asks what happens when the scale cannot answer that

Stevens, S. S. (1946). On the theory of scales of measurement. Science, 103(2684), 677–680.

II · Building a comparison with ggplot

Start with bars

ggplot(states, aes(median_income_real, state)) +
  geom_col()
  • Income on x and state on y: horizontal, so 51 names can be read without turning your head
  • The order is alphabetical, from the bottom up, because state is text. Wyoming is on top
  • Where does Texas stand? You have to read every label to find out

Sort it

states |>
  mutate(state = fct_reorder(state, median_income_real)) |>
  ggplot(aes(median_income_real, state)) +
  geom_col()
  • fct_reorder() turns the text into a factor: a categorical variable that carries its own order, here set by income
  • The lowest income becomes the first level, and the y axis counts up from the bottom, so the richest state lands on top
  • With that one line the shape appears: the richest median is about twice the poorest

Mark one

states |>
  mutate(state = fct_reorder(state, median_income_real)) |>
  ggplot(aes(median_income_real, state,
             fill = state == "Texas")) +
  geom_col() +
  scale_fill_manual(values = c("grey75", dsvar_accent()))
  • state == "Texas" makes two groups, Texas and the rest. scale_fill_manual() gives the rest grey and Texas the one colour
  • Last week’s search: one coloured mark among grey ones is found at once, however many there are. Du Bois’s red bar works the same way
  • Texas is 31st of 51, tied with North Dakota

Take out what carries nothing

states |>
  mutate(state = fct_reorder(state, median_income_real)) |>
  ggplot(aes(median_income_real, state,
             fill = state == "Texas")) +
  geom_col() +
  scale_fill_manual(values = c("grey75", dsvar_accent())) +
  theme(legend.position = "none",
        panel.grid.major.y = element_blank())
  • The legend explained the colour as TRUE and FALSE. The title will do that better
  • Lines between sorted bars carry nothing: the bars are the lines
  • theme() changes ink, never data. Nothing in it can move a bar

Add the claim

states |>
  mutate(state = fct_reorder(state, median_income_real)) |>
  ggplot(aes(median_income_real, state,
             fill = state == "Texas")) +
  geom_col() +
  scale_fill_manual(values = c("grey75", dsvar_accent())) +
  labs(title = "Texas sits in the bottom half",
       subtitle = "Median household income, 2025 dollars",
       caption = paste("n = 51 · Census survey, via FRED",
                       "· margins of error not shown"),
       x = NULL, y = NULL) +
  theme(legend.position = "none",
        panel.grid.major.y = element_blank())
  • The title says the finding, not the variable. It is the line most people read
  • The caption carries what every chart in this course carries: the n, the source, and what the chart cannot tell you

Or draw dots

states |>
  mutate(state = fct_reorder(state, median_income_real)) |>
  ggplot(aes(median_income_real, state,
             colour = state == "Texas")) +
  geom_point(size = 2) +
  scale_colour_manual(values = c("grey60", dsvar_accent())) +
  theme(legend.position = "none")
  • A dot is read by its position, a bar by its length. William Cleveland proposed this dot chart in 1984, after measuring how well people read each
  • Dots use less ink, so many rows stay readable. The order and the finding do not change
  • Part IV asks when the difference between length and position matters

III · What the eye can compare

Side by side: two panels

cells <- mp2 |>
  group_by(share, likes) |>
  summarise(n = n(), mean = mean(norm),
            se = sd(norm) / sqrt(n)) |>
  mutate(lo = mean - 1.96 * se, hi = mean + 1.96 * se)

ggplot(cells, aes(share, mean)) +
  geom_pointrange(aes(ymin = lo, ymax = hi)) +
  facet_wrap(~ likes, labeller = label_both)
  • Six means: three shares, each with few or many likes. facet_wrap() puts the likes in separate panels
  • Within a panel the shares are easy to compare: three dots climbing
  • Across panels it is hard: few against many likes at 25 per cent sends your eye from one panel to the other

Gleicher, M., Albers, D., Walker, R., Jusufi, I., Hansen, C. D., & Roberts, J. C. (2011). Visual comparison for information visualization. Information Visualization, 10(4), 289–309.

Together: one panel

ggplot(cells, aes(share, mean, colour = likes)) +
  geom_pointrange(aes(ymin = lo, ymax = hi),
                  position = position_dodge(width = .4)) +
  scale_colour_manual(values = c("grey60", dsvar_accent()))
  • colour = likes and position_dodge() put few and many likes next to each other at every share
  • Now the comparison the eye makes first is few against many likes, and the answer is plain: at every share the two overlap
  • These are the six means from the last slide. Only where they sit has changed, and with it the comparison the eye makes

Or draw the difference itself

cells |>
  pivot_wider(id_cols = share, names_from = likes,
              values_from = c(mean, se)) |>
  mutate(diff = mean_many - mean_few,
         se   = sqrt(se_many^2 + se_few^2)) |>
  ggplot(aes(diff, share)) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = 0, colour = "grey60") +
  geom_pointrange(aes(xmin = diff - 1.96 * se,
                      xmax = diff + 1.96 * se)) +
  labs(x = "Many likes minus few likes", y = NULL)
  • Compute the comparison and draw that: many minus few likes, at each share, with its 95 per cent interval
  • Every interval crosses zero: the likes made no measurable difference
  • A point, an interval and a line at zero is a coefficient plot. Weeks 3 and 4 use it for every model result

Principle 3, for comparisons

Put what is compared next to each other, on one scale
  • Last week’s ranking of channels: position on a common scale is read most accurately. So a comparison is easiest when both things sit on one axis, side by side
  • Every arrangement makes one comparison easy and the others hard. Decide which comparison is the point, then put those marks together
  • When the difference is the finding, draw the difference. Do not leave the reader to subtract

The same cut, with dots

  • The same cut axis, now with dots, only zooms in: a dot is read by its position, and nothing is drawn as a length
  • Claus Wilke calls the bar rule proportional ink: the amount of ink must match the value it stands for
  • A cut axis on dots still needs saying, and the axis labels say it

Length needs zero. Position does not.

Wilke, C. O. (2019). Fundamentals of Data Visualization, ch. 17, “The principle of proportional ink”. O’Reilly.

Where zero means nothing

PISA’s zero is not no mathematics: the scale was set so that OECD countries averaged 500. Singapore’s 575 is not 24 per cent more mathematics than the United States’ 465. An interval scale, from part I: differences mean something, ratios do not, so a bar’s length has nothing to say.

A scale that starts at 1

Social science papers are full of bars of 1-to-7 means. The first unit of every bar stands for an answer nobody could give, and the bars invite how many times questions the scale cannot answer. Dots can start where the data is, so the intervals become visible: each mean is pinned down to about a tenth of a point either way.

Setting 1 · Where the axis starts

Bars start at a zero that means none. Dots can start anywhere, and say where
  • Ask of any chart: where does the axis start, and does zero on this scale mean none?
  • Zero means none for counts, money and shares of people. It does not for PISA scores, 1-to-7 ratings or temperatures in °C: draw those as dots
  • Week 5 comes back to this for time series, where the zero is a year or an average that somebody chose

V · How it is sorted

We sorted the states by value in one line. Some categories should not be sorted that way.

Text sorts 25 before 5

shares <- c("5%", "25%", "90%")
sort(shares)
[1] "25%" "5%"  "90%"
factor(shares, levels = shares)
[1] 5%  25% 90%
Levels: 5% 25% 90%
  • Text is sorted one character at a time, and “2” comes before “5”: 25% before 5%. ggplot sorts text the same way
  • factor(..., levels = ) writes the right order down. Part II started with that line

When not to sort by value

An order of its own

  • 5, 25 and 90 per cent; agreement from 1 to 7; age groups; months
  • Sorting these by value scrambles the order the data has. Mayr kept his months in a circle for this reason

A lookup

  • A long table the reader searches for one row they already know, such as their own country
  • Alphabetical is the fastest way to find it

Many panels

  • Small multiples share one order, set once
  • A row stays in the same place from panel to panel, so the eye can follow it

By value is the default. Leaving it is a decision, so it needs a reason.

Setting 2 · How it is sorted

By value, unless the categories have an order of their own
  • Ask of any chart: why is it in this order? If nobody chose it, it is probably alphabetical, and alphabetical hides the finding
  • Sorting turns a comparison into a ranking, and a ranking looks more precise than its data. PISA’s 34th was a range from 26th to 38th
  • Sorted by which value is a choice too. Rank the same countries on another index and they move

VI · Who is in it

Du Bois chose nations. Every chart chooses its rows, and most do not say so.

Who is Texas compared to?

The dataset, the column, the sort and the zero are the same on both sides. Texas is 31st of 51 on the left and first on the right. One line chose the rows: filter(state %in% poorest). It does not look like a choice, which makes it an easy way to rig a comparison.

A group is a decision

No column in the dataset puts states into groups: each state occurs once. The census’s four regions make groups, and choosing them was a decision: another map would give other means. One dot per state; the green mark is the region’s mean, with its n.

Everything else is “Other”

states |>
  mutate(group = fct_lump_n(state, n = 5,
                            w = median_income_real)) |>
  group_by(group) |>
  summarise(income = mean(median_income_real)) |>
  ggplot(aes(income, fct_reorder(group, income))) +
  geom_col()
  • fct_lump_n() keeps the five richest states by name and folds the other 46 into one level, Other
  • Other gets a bar like the rest, so it reads as a sixth state at $86k
  • One dot per state, and Other is 46 states, from Mississippi at $58k to District of Columbia at $108k. Its bar was their mean
  • Say what is in Other and how many, or show its members

Setting 3 · Who is in it

Choose who is compared from the question, before you see the result, and say who they are
  • Ask of any chart: who is on it, and who chose them? Then: who is inside each value?
  • Du Bois chose openly, from his question. The rigged version chooses after seeing the result: part VIII has one
  • Groups you make, such as regions, Other or EU or not, decide who is compared with whom. Name them in the subtitle, with their n

VII · Per what

Every number in a comparison has been divided by something, and the divisor is part of the argument.

Every comparison divides by something

  • A rate divides by people: deaths per 100,000, votes per resident. Without it, a map of counts is a map of where people live. Week 6 comes back to this
  • Money divides by prices. “Real” dollars are old dollars divided by a price index, to make them comparable with today’s
  • A ratio divides by the other group. “Seven times the rate of white Americans” is 56.8 divided by 7.7

Dollars of which year?

states_all |>
  filter(state == "Texas",
         year %in% c(1984, 2025)) |>
  select(year, median_income_nominal,
         median_income_real)
# A tibble: 2 × 3

1  1984                 23020              63590
2  2025                 85060              85060
  • In 1984 Texas’s middle household earned $23,020 in the dollars of the time, worth $63,590 in 2025 dollars
  • The real column divides by prices: nominal × price index of 2025 ÷ price index of that year

Seven times, or 49 points?

The census series from the intro, in 1890 and 1930. Subtract, as the intro did, and the gap in illiteracy fell from 49 points to 13. Divide, and Black Americans were still 5.5 times as likely to be unable to read, down from 7.4. Both are true. A difference answers how many more people, a ratio how many times as likely. Say which one you drew. NCES (1993), table 6.

Setting 4 · Per what

Name what each number was divided by: people, prices, or the other group
  • Ask of any chart: what was each number divided by, and would another divisor change the story?
  • Compare counts only when the groups are the same size, which they almost never are
  • Say points or times, never just more

  • filter() chose five of the poorest states as company: who is in it
  • coord_cartesian() cut the axis at $50k, so a small lead looks large: where the axis starts
  • Among all 51, from zero, Texas is 31st
  • The tell: no reason for these six outside the chart. Ask which states the author would have picked before seeing the result

Michael Correll, after Frankfurt, calls a chart like this bullshit: its numbers are true, and it is indifferent to whether its picture is. Your portfolio twin changes one setting only.

Principle 6 · Compared to what? Say so.

Where the axis starts
Bars from a zero that means none; dots can start anywhere
How it is sorted
By value, unless the categories have an order of their own
Who is in it
Chosen from the question, before the result, and named
Per what
People, prices or the other group, and say which

Four questions to ask of any comparison, your own included.
