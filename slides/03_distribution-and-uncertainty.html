Twin

An album cover from a thesis (1979)

What does this chart show?

Unknown Pleasures sleeve, Joy Division, Factory Records, 1979. Design by Peter Saville, after Harold D. Craft Jr’s 1970 Cornell PhD thesis. © Factory Records / Peter Saville; shown at low resolution for criticism and review.

Who drew it, and what it measured

  • Harold Craft, a PhD student in radio astronomy at Cornell, plotted it for his thesis in 1970. It shows CP 1919, the first pulsar ever found, discovered by Jocelyn Bell Burnell in 1967
  • A pulsar is a collapsed star that spins and sends out a beam of radio waves, like a lighthouse. Each time the beam sweeps past Earth, a radio telescope picks up a short pulse, about once every 1.3 seconds
  • What was measured: the strength of the radio signal reaching the telescope, recorded many times within each turn
  • How to read it: each line is one turn, 80 of them stacked. Left to right is time within the turn; height is the strength of the signal. In 1979 Peter Saville put the plot on Joy Division’s first album, uncredited

Rebuilt in R

pulsar |>
  ggplot(aes(t, pulse, height = value,
             group = pulse)) +
  geom_ridgeline(scale = .14, min_height = -Inf,
                 fill = "black", colour = "white") +
  scale_y_reverse() +
  theme_void() +
  theme(plot.background =
          element_rect(fill = "black"))
  • pulsar has one row per turn and moment: 80 turns of 300 readings each, traced by hand from the sleeve, so not Craft’s own numbers
  • geom_ridgeline(), from the ggridges package, draws one line per turn and lets each hide the ones behind it
  • The tracing is CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 (Zöllner; Þorsteinsson), so this chart is too

One change: the average pulse

pulsar |>
  summarise(mean = mean(value),
            low = quantile(value, .1),
            high = quantile(value, .9), .by = t) |>
  ggplot(aes(t, mean)) +
  geom_ribbon(aes(ymin = low, ymax = high),
              fill = dsvar_accent(), alpha = .25) +
  geom_line(colour = dsvar_accent()) +
  labs(x = "Time within the pulse", y = "Signal",
       title = "One average pulse, and the variation gone")
  • summarise(.by = t) turns 80 pulses into one row per moment: the mean signal, and the band in which the middle 80% of pulses fall
  • The band says that the pulses vary, and not how. On the sleeve each peak rises and falls; the tallest is about 6 times the lowest
  • For an astronomer in 1970 that variation was the finding. Part VI comes back to this choice

Who gained from globalisation? (2013)

What does this chart show?

Figure 1(a): Global growth incidence curve, 1988–2008, from C. Lakner & B. Milanovic, Global Income Distribution, World Bank Policy Research Working Paper 6719, 2013. © World Bank, CC BY 3.0 IGO.

Where the numbers come from

  • Christoph Lakner and Branko Milanovic, World Bank economists, took household surveys from more than 100 countries, put every income into one currency adjusted for prices, and lined up the world’s people from poorest to richest: once for 1988, once for 2008
  • Left to right: a place in that line-up. At 5, a person is poorer than 95% of the world; at 50, in the middle; at 99, richer than 99%. The line-up is cut into twenty slices of 5%, each one twentieth of the world’s people; the richest slice is split once more into the top 1%
  • Up: how much the average income of a slice grew from 1988 to 2008, after inflation. One dot: the people between the 50th and 55th place had 76% more in 2008 than those in that slice in 1988
  • A slice is a place, not a group of people. Whoever stood at the 80th place in 2008 was mostly not who stood there in 1988

Rebuilt in R

gic |>
  ggplot(aes(x_plotted, growth_pct)) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = 24.34,
             linetype = "dashed", colour = "grey50") +
  geom_line(colour = "grey70") +
  geom_point(colour = dsvar_accent(), size = 3) +
  labs(x = "Place in the world's income line-up, 1988",
       y = "Growth in income, 1988 to 2008 (%)",
       title = "The middle and the very top gained most")
  • gic has 21 rows, one per slice: where it starts and ends, where its dot is drawn, and how much it grew
  • The dots were measured off the published figure, because the World Bank’s dataset can no longer be downloaded
  • Growth peaks at 76% around the middle, drops to 1% at the 75th to 80th place, and reaches 65% for the top 1%

One change: give each slice its width

gic |>
  ggplot() +
  geom_rect(aes(xmin = pop_from, xmax = pop_to,
                ymin = 0, ymax = growth_pct),
            fill = dsvar_accent(), colour = "white") +
  geom_hline(yintercept = 24.34,
             linetype = "dashed", colour = "grey50") +
  labs(x = "Place in the world's income line-up, 1988",
       y = "Growth in income, 1988 to 2008 (%)",
       title = "The trunk is one person in a hundred")
  • geom_rect() draws each slice from where it starts to where it ends, so a bar is as wide as the share of people it stands for
  • Twenty slices are 5% of the world each. The elephant’s trunk, the top 1%, is a sliver
  • A line gives every dot the same weight. In a histogram the width of a bar decides how much of the data it stands for: Part IV

Thirteen trials of one vaccine (1948 to 1980)

What does this chart show?

“Forest Plot” by Wolfgang Viechtbauer, The metafor Package plot gallery. CC BY-NC-SA 4.0, reproduced unmodified. Data: Colditz, G. A., et al. (1994). Efficacy of BCG vaccine in the prevention of tuberculosis. JAMA, 271(9), 698–702.

Who ran them, and how to read it

  • BCG is a vaccine against tuberculosis. Between 1948 and 1980, thirteen trials around the world vaccinated one group and not another, then counted who fell ill: 357,347 people in all
  • In 1994 Graham Colditz and colleagues pooled the trials in a meta-analysis. The figure is drawn by Wolfgang Viechtbauer’s metafor package for R
  • How to read it: each row is one trial. The square is its risk ratio, the TB rate among the vaccinated divided by the rate among the others; left of 1, the vaccine helped. The line is the trial’s 95% interval, and the square’s size is how much the trial counts in the pooled result
  • The diamond pools all thirteen: a risk ratio of 0.49, from 0.34 to 0.70

Rebuilt in R

trials <- bcg |>
  mutate(trial = paste(author, year),
    rr = (tpos / (tpos + tneg)) / (cpos / (cpos + cneg)),
    se = sqrt(1/tpos - 1/(tpos + tneg) +
              1/cpos - 1/(cpos + cneg)),
    lo = rr * exp(-1.96 * se), hi = rr * exp(1.96 * se))

trials |>
  ggplot(aes(rr, fct_rev(fct_inorder(trial)))) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = 1, linetype = "dashed") +
  geom_pointrange(aes(xmin = lo, xmax = hi)) +
  scale_x_log10() +
  labs(x = "Risk ratio, vaccinated against not (log scale)",
       y = NULL, title = "Eight trials clearly found it helped")
  • One row per trial: who ran it, when, how far from the equator, and four counts: ill and well, vaccinated and not
  • mutate() turns the counts into a risk ratio and its 95% interval. fct_inorder() keeps the original’s order, first trial on top
  • 8 of the 13 intervals lie wholly left of 1, and 5 cross it

One change: where the next trial could land

pooled <- rma(log(rr), se^2, data = trials) |>
  predict(transf = exp)

trials |>
  add_row(trial = "Pooled: all 13 trials", rr = pooled$pred,
          lo = pooled$ci.lb, hi = pooled$ci.ub) |>
  add_row(trial = "A 14th trial, predicted", rr = pooled$pred,
          lo = pooled$pi.lb, hi = pooled$pi.ub) |>
  mutate(summary = is.na(year)) |>
  ggplot(aes(rr, fct_rev(fct_inorder(trial)), colour = summary)) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = 2.5, colour = "grey60") +
  geom_vline(xintercept = 1, linetype = "dashed") +
  geom_pointrange(aes(xmin = lo, xmax = hi)) +
  scale_colour_manual(values = c("black", dsvar_accent()),
                      guide = "none") +
  scale_x_log10() +
  labs(x = "Risk ratio, vaccinated against not (log scale)",
       y = NULL, title = "On average it halves TB. A new trial may not")
  • Pooled: rma() from metafor combines the 13 trials into one average effect, 0.49 (0.34 to 0.70)
  • A 14th trial: where one more trial, in a new place, would be expected to land: 0.15 to 1.55, across 1

II · Building a distribution with ggplot

Start with a histogram

ggplot(teens, aes(internet_use)) +
  geom_histogram()
  • One column, mapped to x. geom_histogram() cuts its range into bins and counts the teenagers in each
  • R chose 30 bins and said so only in a message. Each is 3.6 hours wide
  • So the edges of the bars fall between the round numbers people actually gave

Set the bin width

ggplot(teens, aes(internet_use)) +
  geom_histogram(binwidth = 5, boundary = 0,
                 fill = dsvar_accent(), colour = "white") +
  geom_rug(alpha = .1)
  • binwidth = 5: each bar is five hours. boundary = 0 puts the edges on 0, 5, 10, so a reader can say what a bar holds; the white borders keep the bars apart
  • geom_rug() draws every answer as a tick under the axis
  • 67% of teenagers report under 20 hours a week, and a thin tail runs past 60

Or draw a curve

ggplot(teens, aes(internet_use)) +
  geom_density() +
  geom_rug(alpha = .1)
  • A density curve turns each answer into a small hill and adds the hills up. The area under the curve is 1
  • Up is no longer a count. It shows how crowded the answers are at each value
  • The bumps at 10 and 20 hours are the round numbers again. Part V asks how smooth the curve should be

Mark the middle

ggplot(teens, aes(internet_use)) +
  geom_histogram(binwidth = 5, boundary = 0,
                 fill = dsvar_accent(), colour = "white") +
  geom_vline(xintercept = mean(teens$internet_use),
             linetype = "dashed") +
  geom_vline(xintercept = median(teens$internet_use),
             linetype = "dotted")
  • The median, dotted, is the middle teenager: 10 hours. Half report less, half more
  • The mean, dashed, is 14.8 hours. The tail pulls it to the right, and only 41% are above it
  • With a long tail the two describe different teenagers. Say which one you report

Add the claim

ggplot(teens, aes(internet_use)) +
  geom_histogram(binwidth = 5, boundary = 0,
                 fill = dsvar_accent(), colour = "white") +
  geom_vline(xintercept = median(teens$internet_use),
             linetype = "dotted") +
  labs(title = "Half report 10 hours a week or less",
       subtitle = "Hours per week; dotted: median",
       x = NULL, y = "teenagers",
       caption = paste("n = 3,084 · binwidth 5 hours",
                       "· self-reported · synthetic data"))
  • The title says the finding. The caption carries the n, the binwidth, and what the chart cannot tell you: these are answers to a question, not measured time
  • From now on the binwidth goes in the caption. Part IV shows why

IV · How wide the bins are

A histogram is the data after one number you chose, and a finished chart rarely prints it.

Bins that miss the answers

  • self_esteem takes only 13 values, 0.75 apart. Bins of 0.5 cannot line up with them, and every third one falls between two scores
  • None of the 3,036 teenagers is missing. The gaps belong to the bins, and they look like a fact about people
  • Bins as wide as the step between scores: one bar per score, and the gaps are gone

Too wide, and the non-users disappear

  • At binwidth 1 every answer has its own bar, and the 373 teenagers who never use social network sites form a hump of their own
  • At binwidth 3 they share a bar with the occasional users, and the hump is gone: one hill rising to the right
  • The last bar runs from 5.5 to 8.5, past the end of the scale. A wide bin can hold answers nobody could give

Move it yourself

Setting 1 · How wide the bins are

Bins that follow the answers. Try several, and say which you kept
  • Ask of any histogram: how wide are the bins, and where do their edges fall?
  • Match the bins to the answers people could give: one bar per score on a scale, round numbers for hours. Too fine draws the questionnaire; too wide draws one hill
  • Put the binwidth in the caption. Week 5 meets the same choice again, as how far a smoothed line looks either side

V · How smooth the curve is

A density curve has bins too. You just cannot see them.

The same answers, three curves

  • geom_density(adjust = ) sets how far each answer is spread. 1 is R’s default, 0.4 narrower, 2 wider
  • At 0.4 there is a peak on every multiple of five. At 2 they are gone, and one smooth hill is left
  • All three draw the same 3,063 answers of 70 hours or fewer, from the synthetic survey. None of them is the true shape

People say twenty

Asked how many hours a week, people answer 5, 10, 20 or 30, rarely 17 or 23: nobody keeps count, so they estimate, and estimates come out round. 55% of these answers are multiples of five, where exact answers would give 20% (n = 3,063, up to 70 hours, synthetic data). A narrow curve draws that rounding; a wide one draws the internet use behind it.

A curve that runs below zero

  • Hours cannot be negative. The default curve spreads the answers at 0 to both sides, and 4.7% of its area ends up below zero
  • geom_density(bounds = c(0, Inf)) tells the curve where the scale stops
  • Rating scales have the same edge at both ends. Part VI meets it again in the mean

Setting 2 · How smooth the curve is

A density still has bins. Name the bandwidth, and draw the rug
  • Ask of any density curve: how smooth was it made, and does it stop where the scale stops?
  • A smooth curve looks as if nobody chose anything. It makes the same choice as a histogram, out of sight
  • Draw the rug or the histogram underneath, so the reader can check the curve against the answers

VI · What stands in for the data

Every summary keeps part of the data and drops the rest. Which part is a choice.

From every answer to one number

The same 2,936 answers to the SNS question, five times. Read from the bottom up: each row keeps less. The two humps survive the bottom two rows, and by the box they are gone.

How a box is drawn

John Tukey proposed the box plot in 1977. The box holds the middle half of the answers and the line in it is the median. The whiskers run to the last answer within one and a half box-widths of the box, and anything beyond is drawn as a point. The 1.5 is Tukey’s rule of thumb. A point beyond it is not wrong, and not unusual by any other test.

What a box cannot see

Here the quartiles are 3, 5 and 5, so the median sits on the box’s right edge and the box has no upper half. The 373 teenagers who never use social network sites end up at the tip of the lower whisker, as if they were a thin tail. The counts show them as a group of their own.

Whose number is 4.08?

The mean of all 2,936 answers is 4.08. It lies where the weight balances, and here few teenagers are: 15.8% answered within half a point of it. A mean describes the data well when the data has one hump in the middle. This data has two.

The mean assumes a shape, and so do your tests

  • Reporting a mean and an SD describes the curve on the left. For these 2,936 answers it puts 15.5% of teenagers above 6, on a scale that stops at 6
Test Assumes about the shape
t test each group roughly normal
ANOVA the same, for more groups
Pearson’s r a straight line, no strong outliers
  • A chart of the data is how you find out whether the test fits

Setting 3 · What stands in for the data

Look at the shape before you summarise it, and show the people with the summary
  • Ask of any summary: what shape is it standing in for, and would that shape change the story?
  • A box hides humps. A mean hides humps, tails and the ends of the scale. Draw the answers behind them, as the raincloud did
  • With a long tail, report the median, or both. Next week a line summarises two variables, and the same question comes back

VII · What the whisker shows

A whisker describes the people, or how sure we are of a number. Most charts do not say which.

If we asked again

Treat the 2,936 teenagers as everyone, which we can because they are synthetic, and survey 100 of them at random. The first survey finds a mean of 4.28, the second 4.29, the third 4.50. Everyone’s mean is 4.08. None of the three surveys did anything wrong.

A thousand surveys

Each bar counts surveys, not teenagers: 99 of the 1,000 found a mean between 4.05 and 4.10. This is the distribution of a mean, and it has one hump although the teenagers have two. How far it spreads, 0.18 either way, is how far one survey’s mean could be off: its standard error.

More people, a narrower hump

  • Each panel: 1,000 surveys of the same population, at a different size
  • With 25 people per survey the means spread 0.38 either way; with 400, 0.09. Sixteen times the people, a quarter of the spread
  • The standard error is the SD of the answers divided by the square root of n. The simulated surveys agree with that formula to within 0.01

A hundred surveys, a hundred intervals

Each survey reports its mean and a 95% interval, the mean plus and minus 1.96 standard errors. 92 of these 100 intervals contain everyone’s mean and 8 miss it. The method misses about one time in twenty, and this run happened to miss 8 times. A single survey cannot tell whether its interval is one of the misses. Cumming (2012) calls this the dance of the intervals.

Five whiskers on one mean

Survey 1 again: 100 teenagers, mean 4.28. The top three whiskers describe the people, and the SD runs 1.78 either way. The bottom two describe the mean: its standard error is 0.18, ten times shorter. All five are correct, and a whisker with no label leaves the reader to guess which one it is.

Predict the whisker

People who do this for a living misread them

  • Belia, Fidler, Williams and Cumming (2005) showed authors from psychology, neuroscience and medical journals two means with error bars, and asked them to move one until the difference was just significant
  • Most placed it wrongly. The two rules people reach for, intervals must not overlap and SE bars must not touch, are both wrong, in opposite directions
  • Correll and Gleicher (2014) found that readers judged means more accurately from shapes that fade out, such as gradients and violins, than from bars with error bars

Belia, S., Fidler, F., Williams, J., & Cumming, G. (2005). Researchers misunderstand confidence intervals and standard error bars. Psychological Methods, 10(4), 389–396. · Correll, M., & Gleicher, M. (2014). Error bars considered harmful. IEEE TVCG, 20(12), 2142–2151.

Draw how likely, not only where it ends

  • The same estimate three times. The whisker has hard ends at the 95% limits
  • The gradient fades with how likely each value is, and marks no cut-off, because there is none
  • Twenty dots, each one twentieth of the chance: count them to read a probability (Kay et al., 2016)
  • The forest plot’s rows were whiskers. Week 4 draws every model result as a point and its 95% interval, and names it

Kay, M., Kola, T., Hullman, J. R., & Munson, S. A. (2016). When (ish) is my bus? CHI ’16, 5092–5103. · Cumming, G. (2012). Understanding the New Statistics. Routledge.

Setting 4 · What the whisker shows

A whisker shows the people or the estimate. Say which
  • Ask of any whisker: is it the range, the box, the SD, the SE or a 95% interval? If the caption does not say, it cannot be read
  • Range, box and SD describe the people and do not shrink with more data. SE and the interval describe the estimate, and shrink with the square root of n
  • An interval covers one kind of uncertainty: which people happened to be asked. Next week adds another, the analysis

  • This is how a t test is usually shown, and as a picture of that test it is correct: two means, their standard errors
  • Look at what it does not show

Every number is right. What can a reader not see?

Two lines did it

teens |>
  ggplot(aes(gender, internet_use)) +
  stat_summary(fun = mean, geom = "col",
               fill = c(dsvar_accent(), "grey75")) +
  stat_summary(fun.data = mean_se, geom = "errorbar",
               width = .2) +
  labs(title = "Girls spend 31% more time online",
       subtitle = "Mean internet use, hours per week, ± 1 SE",
       caption = "n = 3,084 · p < .001",
       x = NULL, y = NULL)
  • stat_summary(geom = "col") put two means where 3,084 teenagers were: what stands in for the data
  • mean_se drew one standard error, right for the test, with nothing to say it is about the mean and not the people: what the whisker shows
  • Keep the test’s result, and show the people with it

The same teenagers, and the same two means with their intervals. 48% of girls report less than the boys’ average. Weissgerber et al. (2015) showed that very different datasets can give identical bar charts of means.
