What the chart is asked to do is what each week’s lecture and work group is about, and the order follows the data: one categorical against one continuous, then one continuous, then two, then time. The grammar of graphics, tidy data and iterative refinement are taught throughout. By the way, each thumbnail you see here is a real ggplot figure and can be recreated.

Part I · Claims and comparisons
Weeks 1 and 2

The first two weeks introduce the idea that a chart makes a claim about data, and then apply it to the simplest case: one categorical variable compared on one continuous variable. Week 1 uses a global temperature record, because the same dataset supports several defensible claims. Week 2 moves to a cross section of countries, compared in bar and dot charts.

01
The argument in the graph→

Twelve estimates of one distance from 1644, and one added line that turns “they disagree” into “they are all wrong”. If one line can do that, a chart is a claim and not a picture of the data.

Comparison & ranking
Every comparison answers ‘compared to what?’, and four settings give the answer. Bars encode length and need a zero; points encode position and do not. Ordering categories as an editorial decision. The comparison set, chosen before the result, and the denominator: prices, people or the weights of an index. Part II · Continuous variables
Weeks 3 and 4

Weeks 3 and 4 move from one continuous variable to two, and the work groups stay with one dataset, Gapminder, for both. Week 3 covers how a single variable is distributed and how the uncertainty of a summary is shown. Week 4 covers how two variables relate. The order follows from the content: a fitted line in week 4 comes with an interval, and week 3 introduces how to read one.

03

The mean hides the distribution, and an error bar means four different things to four different readers. Uncertainty is a design problem, not a statistical afterthought.

Distribution & uncertainty
Four settings decide how much of a distribution a reader sees: the bin, the bandwidth, the summary and the interval. Standard deviation, standard error and confidence intervals, and what each communicates; the point with its 95% interval as the standard form for any estimate. Part III · Time, space and text
Weeks 5 and 6

Weeks 5 and 6 turn to variable types that require their own tools. Time comes first, in week 5, with four weeks of R behind it; the work group returns to the Dutch station records from week 1. Week 6 treats maps, networks and text as extensions of the relationships covered in week 4 rather than as three separate topics.

Change over time
Four dials set every time series: the window, the baseline, the span and the aspect ratio. Why the choice of baseline period is a substantive decision, and why ‘9 of the 10 hottest years’ is weaker evidence than it appears. Two series on one time axis, and the second y axis. Lecture
Structure in space and text
Four settings no map or network prints: what gets the ink, which boundaries, which classes and which layout. Why choropleth maps overstate large, sparsely populated areas, and normalisation as the remedy; the modifiable areal unit problem; why the position of nodes in a force directed layout carries no meaning. The difference between how often a word occurs and how important it is. And Snow’s cholera map, which was published after the pump handle had been removed.

Part IV · Your own data
Weeks 7 and 8

In the last two weeks, students apply what the course has covered to a dataset of their own choosing, either one used in the course or one they bring. Week 7 covers annotation, colour and composition. Week 8 has no sessions: students write the take-home assignment on that dataset and hand it in at the end of the week.

The whole story
Four settings decide what a finished chart says: the title, the annotation, the colour and the order. One file with two true titles; annotation that marks where the evidence is sure; a colour scale chosen by the variable’s type, never carrying a difference alone and checked for colour vision deficiency; and narrative order: the martini glass, the drill-down and the slideshow.
