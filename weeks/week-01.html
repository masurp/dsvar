THE LECTURE

Tuesday · 15:30

The argument in the graph

There is no critique yet, so the lecture opens on three charts from the gallery, each rebuilt in R and changed once to say something new. Then: the grammar of graphics as a way of thinking rather than as syntax; perception as the reason design principles are findings and not taste; integrity; how we will argue about charts all term; and showing uncertainty, which every chart you hand in has to do.

Gallery. Van Langren’s twelve estimates of one distance, from 1644, rebuilt with the answer we know now and drawn on a map. The Hertzsprung–Russell diagram, from Russell’s hand-drawn plate to 67,074 stars. Twenty years of news headlines turning negative, and the 47 outlets behind the average. And the Datasaurus dozen.

THE DATA

On Tuesday, HadCRUT5: the global surface temperature record, 1850 to 2025, 176 annual means with a 95% interval on every one of them. The lecture’s three competing stories all come out of this one file.

On Thursday, KNMI De Bilt: one Dutch weather station, 1901 to now, exactly as the institute publishes it: thirteen lines of notes before the data, temperatures in tenths of a degree, and the twelve months side by side as columns.

Before you plot anything: what is one row, what kind of variable does each column hold, and what can the file not tell you? Finding your way around an unfamiliar dataset is one of the things this course teaches.
