Playfair’s wheat and wages

Two and a half centuries of the price of wheat and the wage of a skilled workman, on one page, by the man who invented the line chart and the bar chart. The chart is famous, but the argument it was drawn for is about how many weeks of work a quarter of wheat cost, and that is the one thing it does not show. In the same pamphlet, Playfair drew the chart that does.

THE ORIGINAL

Chart, Shewing at One View the Price of the Quarter of Wheat, & Wages of Labour by the Week, from the Year 1565 to 1821, Chart No. 1 in William Playfair, A Letter on Our Agricultural Distresses, Their Causes and Remedies (London: William Sams, 1821). Public domain (author died 1823); scan via Wikimedia Commons.

William Playfair invented the line chart and the bar chart in 1786 and the pie chart in 1801. This chart comes late in his career, from a pamphlet addressed to the Lords and Commons on the distress of English agriculture. Its point, in the words of the R package that carries its data, was that working men had never been as well off in what their wages would buy as they had become toward the end of the period.

How to read it

Across: time, 1565 to 1821, in five-year steps, with the reigning monarch in a band along the top and the centuries above that.

Each dark bar is the price of a quarter of wheat, eight bushels, in shillings, averaged over five years.

The red line over the pale blue area is a week’s wage of “a good mechanic”, a skilled workman, also in shillings.

One scale serves both, shillings from 0 to 100 on either side. A price per quarter and a wage per week share the unit and nothing else.

THE CRITIQUE

01 · Describe

A grid in five-year and five-shilling steps from 1565 to 1821. Dark bars, one per five years, hover between 25 and 65 shillings for two centuries and then jump to 99 in 1810. Beneath them a red line over a pale blue area climbs slowly from 5 to 30 shillings and stops in 1810. Reigns along the top, centuries above them, a cartouche with the title in the upper middle. No legend: the line is labelled along its length.

02 · Analyse

Time on the horizontal axis, and two quantities on one vertical scale in the same unit: a price per quarter of wheat as bars, a wage per week as a line with an area under it. The two encodings fit what each series is, a level held over five years and a wage that accumulates. The reigns are a categorical annotation of time, the first of their kind. What the shared scale invites is a comparison of heights, and the two heights do not measure the same thing.

03 · Interpret

The claim is that wages had caught up with the price of bread: the gap between the bars and the line narrows from Elizabeth to George III, so a week’s work bought more wheat at the end than at the start. The reader is asked to see the two series move toward each other.

04 · Judge

A claim about a ratio, drawn as a gap. The vertical distance between a price per quarter and a wage per week has no meaning, so the eye is measuring the wrong thing, and the steep last bars say the opposite of the claim: wheat was dearer than ever. The ratio is in the data but not on the chart, and Playfair knew it: Chart No. 3 of the same pamphlet, below, draws the price of the quarter in days of a mechanic’s wages, and there the argument is visible at a glance. The famous chart is the weaker one.

HOW WE KNOW

The values. Playfair published a table with the pamphlet, but the numbers used here were read off Chart No. 1 itself: digitised for Michael Friendly’s gallery of historical graphics, carried into the Protovis examples, and shipped in the HistData package as Wheat and Wheat.monarchs. The comparison above is the check: a misread value would show as a bar out of place.

The unit. A quarter of wheat is eight bushels. HistData’s help page calls it a “quarter bushel”, which is not what Playfair measured; at 47 shillings a quarter in the 1560s, a quarter bushel would have cost more than a week’s wage several times over.

Chart No. 3 against the data. The prices on No. 3, averaged over quarter-centuries, match the digitised bars to within about two shillings in every period. Its days’ wages do not match a simple division of the digitised weekly wage: for 1565 to 1599 the plate shows about 73 days, while dividing the data, at a six-day week, gives 55; for 1800 to 1821 it shows about 14, while the data give 18, partly because the digitised wages end in 1810, the year of the dearest wheat. Playfair’s table must have used wages other than the line on No. 1, or another working week. The ratio drawn below is the division of the numbers we have. It agrees with No. 3 on the direction and on when the fall happened, not on the day counts.

THE SAME NUMBERS, DIFFERENT CHOICES

His own answer: Chart No. 3

Chart Shewing the Value of the Quarter of Wheat in Shillings & in Days Wages of a Good Mechanic from 1565 to 1821, Chart No. 3 in Playfair (1821). Copperplate with added colour, 13.5 × 28.7 cm. Public domain; scan via Wikimedia Commons, from the copy at Princeton, reduced to 1400 pixels wide.

One quarter-century per block, and two prices for the same quarter of wheat: in shillings, and in days of work. Green while the price in days stood above the price in shillings, pink once it fell below. The shillings rise at the end; the days fall all the way. The same pamphlet, two pages on, makes the argument Chart No. 1 only gestures at.

One line where the original had two, and the claim can be read off it. The fall happens between the late seventeenth century and the middle of the eighteenth, and the dear years after 1795 push it back up only a little. A week’s wage bought more wheat at the end than at the start, which is what Playfair argued and what Chart No. 1 cannot show.

Two scales, any story

Show the R code 
dual <- function(meet) {
  # Rescale wages onto the price axis so the two series touch at `meet`, with
  # the ranges matched: the second axis is a and b, chosen.
  b <- diff(range(both$Wheat)) / diff(range(both$Wages))
  a <- both$Wheat[both$Year == meet] - b * both$Wages[both$Year == meet]
  ggplot(both, aes(Year)) +
    geom_col(aes(y = Wheat), fill = p$muted, width = 4.2) +
    geom_line(aes(y = a + b * Wages), colour = dsvar_accent(), linewidth = 1.1) +
    scale_y_continuous(name = "price, shillings a quarter",
                       sec.axis = sec_axis(~ (. - a) / b, name = "wage, shillings a week")) +
    labs(title = sprintf("Wages catch up with wheat in %d", meet), x = NULL) +
    theme(axis.title.y.right = element_text(colour = dsvar_accent()),
          axis.text.y.right = element_text(colour = dsvar_accent()))
}
dual(1700) | dual(1800)

Playfair kept one scale, the honest choice. Give the wage its own axis, as two series in different units usually get, and where the line meets the bars is set by two numbers somebody chose: the offset and the stretch of the second axis. Both panels are the same 50 pairs of values. Neither crossing means anything.

Two series on one time axis invite the reader to read the gap between them, and the gap between a price and a wage measures nothing. When the claim is about how one quantity stands against another, divide them and draw the result. Playfair, who invented the form that made the first chart possible, drew the second chart himself, two pages later.
