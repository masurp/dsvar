Gun deaths in Florida

The same inverted axis as Iraq’s bloody toll, by a designer who cited it as the inspiration. This time the chart reads as saying the opposite of what its own article said. Read the two pages together.

THE ORIGINAL

“Gun deaths in Florida” by Christine Chan, Reuters, 16 February 2014. Copyright Reuters, reproduced at reduced resolution for criticism and review under the Dutch citation right (Auteurswet art. 15a). Not a free licence: it cannot be reused from here. Reuters no longer serves the graphic; this copy is the one carried in LiveScience’s contemporary write-up, and retains Chan’s byline and the Reuters mark.

Christine Chan’s graphic for Reuters (February 2014), accompanying an article on Florida’s Stand Your Ground law.

How to read it

The horizontal axis is years, 1990 to 2012, with the 2005 Stand Your Ground law marked.

The vertical axis is firearm murders, and it is inverted. Zero is at the top; the numbers increase as you go down.

The area under the line is filled red, so it reads as a pool rather than a trend.

Therefore: where the line appears to fall, deaths rose. After 2005 the shape plunges downward: that is the increase.

THE CRITIQUE

01 · Describe

A filled line chart, red, with years across the bottom and a labelled vertical axis whose values increase downward. A marker indicates 2005. The filled shape is deepest at the far left, narrows through the 1990s to its thinnest around 1999, then deepens sharply after 2005 and stays deep.

02 · Analyse

Quantity is on position and the axis is labelled correctly, so nothing here is factually wrong. But the filled line chart is one of the most conventional forms there is, and its convention is that up means more. Inverting the axis leaves that expectation in place, and now it produces a false answer.

03 · Interpret

What the chart is meant to say is that firearm homicides rose after Stand Your Ground. What a fluent chart reader takes from a glance is that they fell. The article’s own text says they rose, so the graphic and its caption are arguing with each other.

04 · Judge

It fails the test the Iraq chart passes: there is a plausible wrong reading, and it is the one most readers will reach. The inversion was borrowed for the metaphor without the condition that made the metaphor safe: Scarr’s bars cannot be misread as “fewer”, and a descending line can. The chart is not a lie, but it is not defensible either.

THE RECREATION, BOTH WAYS UP

blood <- "#8C1C13"

p_data <- bind_rows(mutate(fl, how = "As published: axis inverted"),
                    mutate(fl, how = "Conventional axis")) |>
  mutate(how = factor(how, levels = c("As published: axis inverted", "Conventional axis")))

base_layers <- list(
  geom_area(fill = blood, alpha = .9),
  geom_vline(xintercept = syg, colour = "#3a3a34", linewidth = .4, linetype = "22"),
  labs(x = NULL, y = "Murders committed with a firearm")
)

# The label is placed at an explicit y per panel rather than at Inf: under
# scale_y_reverse, Inf is the BOTTOM of the frame, so an Inf-anchored
# annotation silently vanished off the top of the inverted plot.
top_of <- max(fl$firearm_murders)
lab <- function(y) annotate("text", x = syg + .4, y = y,
                            label = "Stand Your Ground, 2005", hjust = 0,
                            size = 2.8, colour = "#3a3a34", family = dsvar_font())

# Two different y transforms cannot live on one facetted plot, so these are
# two plots placed side by side. patchwork's guide collection and `&` are
# broken against ggplot2 4.x, so each is themed on its own. See the README.
p1 <- ggplot(fl, aes(year, firearm_murders)) + base_layers + lab(60) +
  scale_y_reverse(expand = expansion(mult = c(.08, 0))) +
  labs(title = "As published: axis inverted",
       subtitle = "The shape plunges after 2005: that is the increase")

p2 <- ggplot(fl, aes(year, firearm_murders)) + base_layers + lab(top_of * .97) +
  scale_y_continuous(expand = expansion(mult = c(0, .08))) +
  labs(title = "Conventional axis",
       subtitle = "The same numbers, reading the way readers expect")

library(patchwork)
p1 + p2

WHAT THE NUMBERS ACTUALLY DO

Period Mean firearm murders per year Change
1999–2004 (before) 526 —
2006–2012 (after) 732 +39%

Firearm murders in Florida averaged about 526 a year in the six years before the law and about 732 a year in the seven years after, a rise of roughly 39%. The published chart shows that rise as a downward plunge.

WHAT THIS PAGE IS NOT CLAIMING

That Stand Your Ground caused the increase. A before-and-after difference on one state with no control group cannot establish that, and this page is about an encoding, not a causal question. Researchers who have taken the causal question seriously do report increases (see RAND’s review), but the chart here is not the evidence for it, and neither is the table above.

A CHART THAT SHOWS IT BETTER

Turning the axis the right way up fixes the direction, but the chart still counts murders in a state that grew by 5,900,000 people over these years. Per 100,000 residents, and with the murders that were not committed with a gun drawn beside the ones that were, the picture is sharper than either version above.

Show the R code 
rates <- fl |>
  mutate(Firearm = firearm_murders / population * 1e5,
         `Other means` = (total_murders - firearm_murders) / population * 1e5) |>
  pivot_longer(c(Firearm, `Other means`), names_to = "means", values_to = "rate")

# The averages the table above compares, as segments over the years they cover
periods <- rates |>
  mutate(period = case_when(year %in% 1999:2004 ~ "before",
                            year %in% 2006:2012 ~ "after")) |>
  filter(!is.na(period)) |>
  summarise(rate = mean(rate), from = min(year), to = max(year), .by = c(means, period))
change <- periods |>
  select(means, period, rate) |>
  pivot_wider(names_from = period, values_from = rate) |>
  mutate(pct = 100 * (after - before) / before)
gun_up   <- change$pct[change$means == "Firearm"]
other_dn <- -change$pct[change$means == "Other means"]

cols <- c(Firearm = dsvar_accent(), `Other means` = "grey55")

ggplot(rates, aes(year, rate, colour = means)) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = syg, colour = "#3a3a34", linewidth = .4, linetype = "22") +
  annotate("text", x = syg + .25, y = 8.2, label = "Stand Your Ground, 2005",
           hjust = 0, size = 3, colour = "#3a3a34", family = dsvar_font()) +
  geom_segment(data = periods, aes(x = from - .4, xend = to + .4, y = rate, yend = rate),
               linewidth = 2.4, alpha = .25, lineend = "butt") +
  geom_line(linewidth = .9) +
  geom_point(size = 1.6) +
  # Labelled at the line ends, so there is no legend to decode
  geom_text(data = filter(rates, year == max(year)),
            aes(label = means), hjust = 0, nudge_x = .35, size = 3.4,
            family = dsvar_font(), fontface = "bold") +
  scale_colour_manual(values = cols, guide = "none") +
  scale_x_continuous(breaks = seq(1990, 2010, 5), expand = expansion(add = c(.5, 3))) +
  scale_y_continuous(limits = c(0, NA), expand = expansion(mult = c(0, .05))) +
  labs(title = sprintf("After 2005, more of Florida's murders were committed with guns: the firearm rate rose %.0f%%, other murders fell %.0f%%",
                       gun_up, other_dn),
       subtitle = "Murders per 100,000 residents. Shaded bars: averages for 1999–2004 and 2006–2012",
       x = NULL, y = "Murders per 100,000 residents",
       caption = sprintf("n = %d years · Florida Department of Law Enforcement, UCR; population from the same series · a before-and-after comparison, not a causal estimate",
                         nrow(fl)))

Three changes from the original, each of which the original’s question needed: the axis is upright and starts at zero; the counts are rates, so population growth is not mistaken for a trend; and the murders committed without a gun are on the same chart, which is the comparison that shows the change was in the weapon more than in the number of killings. The note above still applies: this is a description of what happened after 2005, not evidence about why.

THE MOST USEFUL THING ABOUT THIS CASE

Chan was not hiding anything. The axis was labelled, the article said deaths rose, and she explained afterwards that she preferred to show deaths as descending and had taken the idea from Scarr’s Iraq graphic. Everything about the intent was honest, and the chart still misleads, because a reader spends about four seconds on a graphic and spends them on the shape, not the axis labels. That is why “but it is technically accurate” is not a defence, and why the useful question is never “is this true?” but “what will this be read to say?”
