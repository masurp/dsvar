Income and life expectancy

Four variables in one scatterplot, and a fifth added by making it move. Hans Rosling’s bubble chart is the most-watched statistical graphic of the last twenty years, and almost everything that makes it work is a choice you could have made differently.

THE ORIGINAL

“Gapminder World 2015” (version 15), Gapminder Foundation, CC BY 4.0. Based on a free chart from www.gapminder.org. Reproduced unaltered. Gapminder’s own data lines: income from the World Bank’s GDP per capita (PPP, 2011 international $), population from the UN Population Division, life expectancy from IHME GBD-2015.

Gapminder World was developed by Hans Rosling, Ola Rosling and Anna Rosling Rönnlund, and performed rather than published. This is the still poster, the one thing about Gapminder World that holds still. Most people met it in the 2006 TED talk, a man talking over a moving chart, and the live tool is a third thing again. Which of the three you have in mind changes what the critique below is about.

How to read it

Each bubble is a country. Horizontal position is income per person, vertical position is life expectancy.

The size of the bubble is population, so China and India dominate visually without dominating the trend.

Colour is the region: an unordered grouping, so it gets hue rather than lightness.

The horizontal axis is logarithmic. Equal steps are equal multiples of income, not equal dollars. It is the decision the chart rests on.

In the original it moves, one frame per year, and the fifth variable is time.

Gapminder Foundation. Data and tools at gapminder.org, released CC BY 4.0. The original interactive is not embedded here. The recreation runs on the poster’s own data: Gapminder’s figures for 2015 as they stood in October 2016, in 2011 dollars. Every section after it uses Jenny Bryan’s gapminder extract: 142 countries, every five years from 1952 to 2007.

THE CRITIQUE

01 · Describe

A scatterplot of a few hundred circles. Two positional channels, one size channel, one colour channel and, in the original, an animation control. Both axes are labelled and the horizontal one is unevenly spaced.

02 · Analyse

The two variables the argument is about get position, the most accurate channel, which is right. Population goes on area, which is weak, and that is also right: nobody is asked to compare populations, only to notice that some dots are big. The log axis converts a violently skewed variable into something a straight line can pass through.

03 · Interpret

The claim is that there is no first world and third world, only a continuum. When it moves, it adds that essentially every country is travelling up and to the right. Rosling built it to destroy a specific mental model, and the shape of the cloud is the rebuttal.

04 · Judge

It holds, with one asterisk. The log axis is what makes the relationship look tidy, and a reader who does not notice it will badly underestimate how large the income gaps are in absolute terms. That is a defensible choice, clearly labelled, but the chart is much more persuasive about convergence than the underlying dollars warrant.

THE RECREATION

Show the R code 
# Drawn at the poster's proportions, 3166 x 2238 px, and on the poster's own
# data: Gapminder's 2015 figures as of October 2016, the vintage its footer
# names. The gapminder extract used everywhere else on this page stops in 2007
# and is in older dollars, which put India and Nigeria a whole income level
# below where the poster has them. See R/fetch-gallery-data.R.
w15 <- read_csv(here::here("data", "raw", "gapminder-world-2015.csv"),
                show_col_types = FALSE)
tok <- dsvar_tokens()

# The poster's four regions, each in the palette colour nearest the poster's:
# Africa blue, the Americas green, Asia red, Europe yellow.
region_pal <- c(africa = dsvar_cat[[2]], americas = dsvar_cat[[1]],
                asia   = dsvar_cat[[3]], europe   = dsvar_cat[[7]])

# Gapminder's four income levels. The poster prints no numbers for them, so the
# cut-offs are measured off its pixels: one dashed line sits exactly on $8,000
# and the others are equal steps either side on the log axis, a factor of about
# 2.94 each. Each label goes in the middle of its band, as on the poster.
level_step  <- 2.94
level_lines <- 8000 * level_step^(-1:2)     # about $2,700, $8,000, $23,500, $69,000
level_mids  <- 8000 * level_step^(-1.5:1.5)

# Bubble AREA proportional to population, starting from zero, which is what
# scale_size_area() does and what the poster does. Plain scale_size() rescales
# from the smallest country, adding a floor that makes 10 million look barely
# bigger than 1 million; the size key below would then be drawing a lie.
# scale_radius() would map radius instead and square the impression — the same
# trap as Nightingale. The chart and its key share this one scale, so the key's
# circles are the chart's circles. max_bubble is measured too: it makes the
# 1,000 million circle as wide, for the width of the chart, as the poster's
# (251 px of 3166).
max_bubble <- 31
pop_scale  <- scale_size_area(max_size = max_bubble,
                              limits = c(0, max(w15$population)), guide = "none")

# A few countries named, to anchor the cloud: the two giants, a spread of rich
# ones, the two ends, and the ones that sit far from the curve, like South
# Africa, which is about as rich as Brazil and lives thirteen years less.
# Every country gets a row here, most with an empty label, so the labels are
# pushed clear of all 193 bubbles and not only the eleven named ones. point.size
# is each bubble's drawn size, worked out the way pop_scale does it, so a label
# cannot land inside India or China. The names are the poster's.
named <- w15 |>
  mutate(label = case_match(country,
                            c("China", "India", "Japan", "Germany", "Russia",
                              "Brazil", "Nigeria", "South Africa", "Qatar") ~ country,
                            "United States" ~ "USA",
                            "Central African Republic" ~ "Central African Rep.",
                            .default = ""),
         drawn = max_bubble * sqrt(population / max(population)))

# A dark hairline round each bubble, as on the poster: a white one swallows the
# smallest countries whole, and shows nothing where two bubbles overlap.
bubbles <- ggplot(w15, aes(gdp_per_capita, life_expectancy)) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = level_lines, linetype = "22",
             colour = tok$muted, linewidth = .35) +
  geom_point(aes(size = population, fill = region), shape = 21,
             colour = alpha(tok$ink, .45), stroke = .25, alpha = .85) +
  ggrepel::geom_text_repel(data = named,
                           aes(gdp_per_capita, life_expectancy, label = label,
                               point.size = drawn),
                           inherit.aes = FALSE, size = 3.1, family = dsvar_font(),
                           colour = tok$ink_2, seed = 4,
                           box.padding = .4, min.segment.length = 0,
                           max.overlaps = Inf, segment.size = .25,
                           segment.colour = "grey55",
                           bg.colour = tok$paper, bg.r = .12) +
  # The poster's frame, read off it like the levels: $574 to $144,000, widened
  # here to $150,000 so Macao ($148,000), which the poster leaves out, stays
  # in; 46.5 to 85 years. Breaks in doublings, as on the poster, which is what
  # puts $8,000 on a level line. The level names ride on a second axis along
  # the top, where the poster has them.
  scale_x_log10(breaks = 1000 * 2^(0:7), limits = c(574, 150000),
                expand = expansion(0), labels = scales::label_dollar(),
                sec.axis = dup_axis(name = NULL, breaks = level_mids,
                                    labels = paste("Level", 1:4))) +
  scale_y_continuous(breaks = seq(50, 85, 5), limits = c(46.5, 85),
                     expand = expansion(0)) +
  pop_scale +
  scale_fill_manual(values = region_pal, guide = "none") +
  labs(title = "Richer countries live longer, and the gain flattens out",
       subtitle = "Each bubble is a country. Dashed lines split income into Gapminder's four levels; note the log axis",
       x = "GDP per person (2011 dollars, adjusted for prices; log scale)",
       y = "Life expectancy (years)",
       caption = sprintf("n = %d countries, 2015 · Gapminder, data as of October 2016 · income levels measured off the poster",
                         nrow(w15))) +
  theme(axis.text.x.top = element_text(colour = tok$ink_3, face = "bold",
                                       size = rel(.85), margin = margin(b = 4)))

# THE KEY, after the poster's: a map that is the colour legend, and four
# circles that are the size legend. It is one ggplot in map coordinates
# (Robinson, in metres), with the row of circles placed under the map in the
# same units. Each country on the map takes its region from the data, so the
# map agrees with the bubbles (Russia and Turkey are Europe, Australia is Asia).
# The few Natural Earth shapes with no row, like Western Sahara and Taiwan,
# fall back on their UN region, with Oceania folded into Asia as Gapminder
# does. Antarctica has no region, so it goes.
fallback <- c(Africa = "africa", Americas = "americas", Asia = "asia",
              Europe = "europe", Oceania = "asia")
world <- sf::st_transform(rnaturalearth::countries110, "+proj=robin") |>
  left_join(transmute(w15, ADM0_A3 = toupper(geo), region), by = "ADM0_A3") |>
  mutate(region = coalesce(region, fallback[REGION_UN])) |>
  filter(!is.na(region))

bb <- sf::st_bbox(world)
w  <- bb$xmax - bb$xmin               # everything below is placed in map widths
size_key <- tibble(pop   = c(1, 10, 100, 1000) * 1e6,
                   label = c("1", "10", "100", "1,000"),
                   x     = bb$xmin + w * c(.04, .18, .36, .72))
y_head <- c(bb$ymax + .14 * w, bb$ymin - .07 * w)
y_row  <- bb$ymin - .42 * w
y_lab  <- bb$ymin - .74 * w

key <- ggplot() +
  geom_sf(data = world, aes(fill = region), colour = NA) +
  annotate("text", x = bb$xmin, y = y_head, hjust = 0, vjust = 1,
           label = c("Colour by region", "Size by population, millions"),
           size = 3.2, fontface = "bold", colour = tok$ink_3,
           family = dsvar_font()) +
  geom_point(data = size_key, aes(x, y_row, size = pop), shape = 21,
             fill = tok$hairline, colour = tok$muted, stroke = .3) +
  geom_text(data = size_key, aes(x, y_lab, label = label), size = 3.1,
            colour = tok$ink_3, family = dsvar_font()) +
  scale_fill_manual(values = region_pal, guide = "none") +
  pop_scale +
  coord_sf(xlim = c(bb$xmin, bb$xmax), ylim = c(y_lab - .05 * w, y_head[1]),
           expand = FALSE, datum = NA, clip = "off") +
  theme_void() +
  theme(plot.background = element_rect(fill = tok$surface, colour = tok$hairline,
                                       linewidth = .5),
        plot.margin = margin(10, 12, 10, 12))

# Into the empty lower right, where the poster puts it, over the last level
# line as on the poster. No country sits under it.
bubbles + patchwork::inset_element(key, left = .78, bottom = .03, right = .977,
                                   top = .55, align_to = "panel")

THE DECISION THE CHART RESTS ON

Take the log axis away and change nothing else.

Show the R code 
# The transform has to be applied to the VALUE, not requested via scale_x_log10:
# a scale applies to the whole plot, so one facet cannot be log and the other
# linear. The first version of this figure used scale_x_log10 outside the facet
# and silently drew the identical linear panel twice.
both <- bind_rows(
  latest |> mutate(x = gdpPercap,        scale = "Linear income axis"),
  latest |> mutate(x = log10(gdpPercap), scale = "Log income axis")
) |> mutate(scale = factor(scale, levels = c("Linear income axis", "Log income axis")))

ggplot(both, aes(x, lifeExp, size = pop, fill = continent)) +
  geom_point(shape = 21, colour = "white", stroke = .25, alpha = .85) +
  facet_wrap(~scale, scales = "free_x") +
  scale_size(range = c(1, 11), guide = "none") +
  scale_fill_manual(values = cont_pal, name = NULL) +
  labs(x = "GDP per person · left: dollars · right: log10(dollars)",
       y = "Life expectancy (years)",
       title = "Same 142 countries, same year, one axis changed",
       subtitle = "On the left almost every country is crushed against the axis; on the right the relationship appears",
       caption = sprintf("n = %d countries, %d · Gapminder", nrow(latest), max(gap$year))) +
  theme(legend.position = "bottom")
Show the R code 
inc <- bind_rows(
  mutate(latest, how = "Income, linear", v = gdpPercap),
  mutate(latest, how = "Income, log10",  v = log10(gdpPercap))
) |> mutate(how = factor(how, levels = c("Income, linear", "Income, log10")))

ggplot(inc, aes(v)) +
  geom_histogram(bins = 30, fill = dsvar_accent(), colour = "white", linewidth = .2) +
  facet_wrap(~how, scales = "free") +
  labs(x = NULL, y = "Countries",
       title = "Why the log axis is not a trick",
       subtitle = "Income across countries is roughly log-normal; the transform is the shape of the variable, not a flattering choice",
       caption = sprintf("n = %d countries, %d", nrow(latest), max(gap$year)))

WHAT THE LOG AXIS GIVES AND TAKES

It gives a readable relationship, because income across countries is close to log-normal and the transform turns a wall of points into a line. It takes away your sense of absolute distance: on that axis the gap between $500 and $5,000 looks the same as the gap between $5,000 and $50,000, and those are not the same thing to live through. Both are true at once. State the axis, and expect readers to misjudge the dollars anyway.

WHAT THE ANIMATION WAS DOING

The famous version moves. Here the same fifth variable is drawn as small multiples, the static answer to animation.

Show the R code 
ggplot(gap, aes(gdpPercap, lifeExp, size = pop, fill = continent)) +
  geom_point(shape = 21, colour = "white", stroke = .15, alpha = .8) +
  facet_wrap(~year, ncol = 6) +
  scale_x_log10(labels = scales::label_number(scale_cut = scales::cut_short_scale())) +
  scale_size(range = c(.4, 5), guide = "none") +
  scale_fill_manual(values = cont_pal, name = NULL) +
  labs(x = "GDP per person (log scale)", y = "Life expectancy (years)",
       title = "Every country moving up and to the right, 1952 to 2007",
       caption = sprintf("n = %d countries × 12 years · Gapminder", length(unique(gap$country)))) +
  theme_dsvar(base_size = 8.5) +
  theme(legend.position = "bottom")

The small multiples are better for anything you want to check: you can compare 1972 with 1992 by moving your eyes, which an animation makes impossible. The animation is better for the thing Rosling actually wanted, which was for you to feel a world in motion and abandon a category you had been carrying since school. Neither is the correct chart. They are answers to different questions, and only one of them can be argued with.
