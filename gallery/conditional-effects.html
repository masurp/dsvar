Conditional effects

The plot you reach for when an effect is supposed to depend on something else. It comes in two flavours (sample the moderator at three points, or draw the effect across all of it), and they lead readers to different conclusions from the same model. This one is from my own data, and the choices below are ones I actually had to make.

THE ORIGINAL

Figure 3 from Masur, P. K., & Parry, D. A. (2026). When sharing becomes the norm: How disclosure prevalence and relational similarity shape social norms on social media. Technology, Mind, and Behavior. © American Psychological Association; reproduced here by one of its authors for teaching. Data: osf.io/zw6pb.

Study 1 sat participants in front of a simulated social feed in which the share of personal disclosures was high or low, and afterwards asked them what they thought was normal on that platform and how likely they were to post something personal themselves. The question the figure answers is whether that chain runs differently for people who felt similar to the others in the feed.

How to read it

The x axis is the moderator: felt similarity, mean-centred, so 0 is the average participant.

The y axis is an effect, not an outcome: the slope you would get if you ran the analysis on people at that value of the moderator. That is what makes the genre hard: every point on the line is a whole regression coefficient.

The horizontal dashed line is zero, i.e. no effect. Where the ribbon crosses it, the effect is no longer distinguishable from nothing.

The vertical dashed line is the Johnson–Neyman point: the value of the moderator at which the interval first excludes zero. Teal to the right of it, red to the left: significant, then not.

Panels A–C are the visual-disclosure route, D–F the written one: the effect of the feed on what people think is normal (path a), the effect of that perception on their own intention (path b), and the indirect effect, the two multiplied.

THE RECREATION

The top row of the original, rebuilt from the open data in ggplot. Two changes: the x axis carries the raw 1–7 similarity scale rather than the centred one, because “similarity = 0” is not a thing a reader can picture, and the significant/not split is stated in the legend rather than left to be inferred from two colours.

Show the R code 
# The indirect effect is a product of two estimated slopes, so its interval is
# not a delta-method sum. Percentile bootstrap: resample participants, refit
# both equations, multiply, and read the quantiles off the 1000 curves.
set.seed(2026)
boot <- replicate(1000, {
  s  <- d1[sample.int(nrow(d1), replace = TRUE), ]
  fa <- lm(norm ~ prev_vis * sim_c + prev_wri, data = s)
  fb <- lm(intention ~ norm * sim_c + inj + prev_vis + prev_wri, data = s)
  (coef(fa)[["prev_vis"]] + coef(fa)[["prev_vis:sim_c"]] * w_grid) *
  (coef(fb)[["norm"]]     + coef(fb)[["norm:sim_c"]]     * w_grid)
})

panels <- c(
  "A · Path a: effect of the feed on what people think is normal",
  "B · Path b: effect of that perception on their own intention",
  "C · a × b: the indirect effect of the feed on intention")

ce <- bind_rows(
  cond_effect(m_a, "prev_vis", "prev_vis:sim_c", w_grid) |> mutate(panel = panels[1]),
  cond_effect(m_b, "norm",     "norm:sim_c",     w_grid) |> mutate(panel = panels[2]),
  tibble(w   = w_grid,
         est = (coef(m_a)[["prev_vis"]] + coef(m_a)[["prev_vis:sim_c"]] * w_grid) *
               (coef(m_b)[["norm"]]     + coef(m_b)[["norm:sim_c"]]     * w_grid),
         lwr = apply(boot, 1, quantile, .025),
         upr = apply(boot, 1, quantile, .975),
         panel = panels[3])
) |>
  mutate(panel      = factor(panel, levels = panels),
         similarity = w + mean_sim,
         sig = if_else(lwr > 0 | upr < 0, "95% CI excludes zero", "95% CI includes zero"))

# The Johnson-Neyman point is simply where that label changes.
jn <- ce |>
  group_by(panel) |>
  filter(sig != lag(sig, default = first(sig))) |>
  slice(1) |>
  ungroup()

pal_sig <- c("95% CI excludes zero" = dsvar_cat[1],
             "95% CI includes zero" = dsvar_cat[3])

ggplot(ce, aes(similarity, est)) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = 0, linewidth = .4, colour = dsvar_light$hairline) +
  geom_ribbon(aes(ymin = lwr, ymax = upr, fill = sig), alpha = .16) +
  geom_line(aes(colour = sig, group = 1), linewidth = .9) +
  geom_vline(data = jn, aes(xintercept = similarity), linetype = "22",
             linewidth = .4, colour = dsvar_light$muted) +
  facet_wrap(~panel, scales = "free_y", labeller = label_wrap_gen(34)) +
  scale_colour_manual(values = pal_sig, name = NULL) +
  scale_fill_manual(values = pal_sig, guide = "none") +
  labs(x = "Felt similarity to the people in the feed (1–7)",
       y = "Conditional effect") +
  theme(legend.position = "bottom")

n = 589 participants. Path a from norm ~ prevalence * similarity, path b from intention ~ norm * similarity, both holding the written-disclosure manipulation constant and path b also holding the injunctive norm constant. Ribbons are 95% intervals: delta method for a and b, 1000-sample percentile bootstrap for the product. Dashed verticals mark the Johnson–Neyman points.

CHECKED AGAINST THE PUBLISHED FIGURE

The shapes match and the moderation is the same in sign and size, but the recreation sits a little higher than the printed panels and its cut points sit further left. Published panel A runs from about 2.26 down to 1.06 across the similarity range; ours runs from 2.65 to 1.49: the same −0.2 per scale point, shifted up. Published panel B turns significant at about −1.6 centred; ours at about −2.2. The paper applies exclusions derived from participants’ browsing logs which are not in the open file, so our 589 is the larger sample. Nothing in the conclusion moves, but the Johnson–Neyman point does, by most of a scale point. The cut point is the least stable number on the whole figure, and it is the one readers quote.

THE SAME MODERATION, PICK-A-POINT

The other way to draw this. Instead of the effect at every value of the moderator, choose a few values (by convention the mean and one standard deviation either side) and plot the ordinary outcome-by-condition picture once per value. It uses the same model and the same data, and estimates nothing new.

Show the R code 
pts <- c(-sd_sim, 0, sd_sim)
lab <- c("low similarity (−1 SD)", "average similarity", "high similarity (+1 SD)")

# Predicted norm perception in each feed, at each of the three chosen values.
pap <- expand_grid(prev_vis = 0:1, sim_c = pts) |>
  mutate(prev_wri = mean(d1$prev_wri))
pap <- bind_cols(pap, as_tibble(predict(m_a, pap, interval = "confidence"))) |>
  mutate(level = factor(lab[match(sim_c, pts)], levels = lab),
         feed  = factor(if_else(prev_vis == 1, "many personal posts", "few personal posts"),
                        levels = levels(d1$feed)))

# The slope of each of those three lines is one conditional effect.
slopes <- cond_effect(m_a, "prev_vis", "prev_vis:sim_c", pts) |>
  mutate(level = factor(lab, levels = lab), similarity = w + mean_sim)
curve_a <- cond_effect(m_a, "prev_vis", "prev_vis:sim_c", w_grid) |>
  mutate(similarity = w + mean_sim)

pal3 <- setNames(dsvar_ramp_light[c(1, 3, 5)], lab)

p_pap <- ggplot(pap, aes(feed, fit, colour = level, group = level)) +
  geom_line(linewidth = .9) +
  geom_linerange(aes(ymin = lwr, ymax = upr), linewidth = .9, alpha = .45) +
  geom_point(size = 2.4) +
  scale_colour_manual(values = pal3, name = NULL) +
  coord_cartesian(ylim = c(1, 7)) +
  labs(title = "Pick-a-point",
       subtitle = "The moderator sampled at three values",
       x = "Feed the participant browsed", y = "Perceived norm (1–7)") +
  theme(legend.position = "bottom")

p_curve <- ggplot(curve_a, aes(similarity, est)) +
  geom_ribbon(aes(ymin = lwr, ymax = upr), fill = dsvar_cat[1], alpha = .14) +
  geom_line(colour = dsvar_cat[1], linewidth = .9) +
  geom_linerange(data = slopes, aes(ymin = lwr, ymax = upr, colour = level),
                 linewidth = 1.1) +
  geom_point(data = slopes, aes(colour = level), size = 2.6) +
  scale_colour_manual(values = pal3, guide = "none") +
  labs(title = "… are three points on this curve",
       subtitle = "Panel A again, with those three values marked",
       x = "Felt similarity (1–7)",
       y = "Effect of the feed on perceived norm")

WHICH ONE TO DRAW

What pick-a-point gets right

Its y axis is a quantity the reader already understands, a score on the scale the study measured, so it can be checked against the data and against common sense. It shows the size of the thing: the feed moves perceived norms by roughly two points on a seven-point scale at every level of similarity, which is large, and the moderation shrinks it by a quarter of a point, which is not. Three near-parallel lines are an honest picture of that.

What pick-a-point hides

The three values are a choice, and the plot does not admit it. ±1 SD is a convention, not a fact about the moderator; on a skewed or bounded moderator the “high” line can sit at a value almost nobody scored. Worse, the picture is unfalsifiable at a glance: three lines that are not quite parallel look like a moderation whether the interaction is significant or noise, which is exactly how a small wobble gets promoted into a finding.

What the conditional-effect plot gets right

It stops choosing. The whole moderator range is on the page, the interval is drawn everywhere, and the reader can see that path a is comfortably non-zero across the entire scale while path b is not. It is also the only one of the two that makes the uncertainty in the interaction visible: the ribbon fans out towards the ends of the scale, where there are few participants, and that fanning is the honest reason not to extrapolate.

What it hides: the significance-region trap

Colouring the line by whether the interval excludes zero invites the reading that the effect switches on at the cut point. It does not. Nothing happens at similarity 2.8; an interval that just includes zero and one that just excludes it are the same estimate with a rounding error between them. And as the check above shows, moving that cut point by most of a scale point took nothing more than a different exclusion rule. Draw the ribbon; be very careful about quoting the number where it crosses.

The verdict

Draw both, in this order. Pick-a-point first, because it answers “how big, on what scale” and keeps the reader anchored to the measurement. The conditional-effect plot second, because it answers “does this hold everywhere” and shows the uncertainty the first one leaves out. Neither is a substitute for the other, and a paper that shows only the second (which is most of them) has told you where an effect is significant without ever telling you how large it is.

WHAT THE MODEL ACTUALLY SAYS

bind_rows(
  broom::tidy(m_a, conf.int = TRUE) |> mutate(equation = "Path a · norm"),
  broom::tidy(m_b, conf.int = TRUE) |> mutate(equation = "Path b · intention")
) |>
  filter(str_detect(term, ":")) |>
  transmute(Equation = equation,
            `Interaction term` = str_replace_all(term, c("prev_vis" = "prevalence",
                                                         "sim_c"    = "similarity",
                                                         "norm"     = "perceived norm")),
            estimate = round(estimate, 3),
            `95% CI` = sprintf("[%.2f, %.2f]", conf.low, conf.high),
            p = round(p.value, 3)) |>
  knitr::kable()

Path a · norm prevalence:similarity -0.193 [-0.36, -0.02] 0.026
Path b · intention perceived norm:similarity 0.053 [0.01, 0.10] 0.016

Both interactions are real but small. The feed’s effect on perceived norms weakens by 0.19 scale points per point of similarity, against a main effect of 1.87, so over the whole observed range the effect shrinks from about 2.6 to 1.5, and never comes close to vanishing. That is the number the pick-a-point panel puts in front of you and the conditional-effect panel makes you infer from an axis of slopes.

THE SENTENCE TO AVOID

“The effect of the feed was significant only for participants high in similarity.” Look at panel A: it is significant everywhere, and it is stronger for participants low in similarity. The sentence is about path b, where the cut point lives, and even there it means “our sample was too small to resolve a small effect down at that end of the scale”, not “the effect is absent there”. A conditional-effect plot is a map of what this study could detect, not of where the effect exists.

THE RULE THIS CASE EXISTS FOR

A conditional-effects plot is a picture of a model, not of the world. Put the effect size on a scale the reader can hold (pick-a-point), then put the uncertainty everywhere it goes, across the whole moderator (the conditional-effect curve). Show one without the other and you have either a moderation nobody can falsify or a cut point nobody should quote.
