Two centuries of extreme poverty

Two hundred years of the world’s population, split into the people living in extreme poverty and everyone else. It answers the objection a falling share always meets, that the world just got bigger, by drawing the count and the total together. It does not show how little is known about its left half.

THE ORIGINAL

“World population living in extreme poverty, 1820 to 2015”, Our World in Data, from the topic page Poverty by Joe Hasell, Bertha Rohenkohl, Pablo Arriagada, Esteban Ortiz-Ospina and Max Roser, OurWorldinData.org/poverty. Licensed CC BY 4.0; reproduced unaltered. Data: Ravallion (2016), updated with World Bank (2019), from Bourguignon & Morrisson (2002) and the World Bank.

The visualisation goes back to Martin Ravallion’s The Economics of Poverty (2016). Our World in Data redrew it and brought it up to 2015 from the same sources: Bourguignon and Morrisson’s reconstruction of the world income distribution for the years before 1981, and the World Bank’s household surveys after. It is one of the most shared charts Our World in Data has made, and one of the most argued over.

How to read it

The red band is the number of people in extreme poverty: living on less than $1.90 a day, in dollars adjusted for prices in each country and for inflation.

The green band is everyone else. It is stacked on the red, so the top edge of the chart is the whole world population.

Only the red band starts from zero. Read the green as the distance between the two edges, not as a height.

The two halves are measured differently. Before 1981 the numbers are reconstructed from historical GDP; from 1981 they come from household surveys. The chart draws them as one surface.

THE CRITIQUE

01 · Describe

A stacked area from 1820 to 2015, red under green, on an axis from 0 to 8 billion. The total climbs from 1.1 to 7.3 billion. The red band grows slowly to about 1.9 billion around 1980, then falls to 0.7 billion. Both bands are labelled at the right; the subtitle defines the poverty line; the footer names the sources and links to a page on their limits.

02 · Analyse

Two counts, stacked, so position on a common scale works for the red band and the total, and the green band is only a difference. Nothing marks where the data is: 10 estimates between 1820 and 1980, joined by straight lines across gaps of up to forty years, then a point every year or three from 1981. The change of method at 1981 is invisible, and so is any uncertainty. The red is the conventional colour for a bad thing and the green for a good one, which tells the reader which way to feel before they read a number.

03 · Interpret

The claim is that for most of modern history most people were extremely poor, that their number kept rising until about 1980, and that it has since fallen by more than half while the world population kept growing. Share and count are both on the page: in 1820 the red band is 89% of the total, in 2015 10%. The design choice that matters is showing counts: the decline cannot be explained away by a bigger denominator.

04 · Judge

A whole-story chart that earns the name: the outcome, the population it belongs to, and two centuries, in one picture with direct labels and a zero baseline. Its weakness is that it is equally confident everywhere. The left half rests on 10 reconstructions from historical GDP, and Our World in Data’s own methods page says “there are substantial questions that introduce uncertainty which need to be taken seriously”. The result also depends on the line: $1.90 a day is very low, and Our World in Data’s current charts use the World Bank’s newer $3 line. A chart that marked its data points and its seam would make the same argument and say how much of it is measured.

HOW WE KNOW

The data is the chart’s own. Our World in Data publishes the numbers behind every chart as a CSV next to it, and that file is what this page draws. Its metadata names the sources and the date it was last changed, 1 February 2019. The series is frozen there: the poverty line is the $1.90 of 2011 prices, which the World Bank has since replaced twice.

Two kinds of number, one surface. Our World in Data’s methods page explains that the estimates before 1981 come from Bourguignon and Morrisson’s “reconstruction of the global income distribution using the Maddison Project’s database of historical GDP per capita series”, and the later ones from household surveys collected by the World Bank. The file has both a 1980 and a 1981 value, one from each:

Show the R code 
pov |>

            `in extreme poverty (bn)` = round(poor / 1e9, 3),

The two methods meet almost exactly, a sign that the joining was done with care. That does not make the left half as well measured as the right.

The same red band as a line, with its data points drawn and the change of method shaded. The shape is the same, but now the reader can see that the slow rise of the nineteenth century is ten numbers, each built from a country’s GDP and an assumed distribution, and that the steep fall after 1990 is measured year by year.

The share instead of the count

Show the R code 
ggplot(pov, aes(year, share)) +
  geom_area(fill = dsvar_accent(), alpha = .18) +
  geom_line(colour = dsvar_accent(), linewidth = 1) +
  geom_point(colour = dsvar_accent(), size = 1.6) +
  annotate("text", x = first$year + 2, y = first$share + .06, hjust = 0, size = 3.4,
           family = dsvar_font(), colour = p$ink_2,
           label = sprintf("%d: %.0f%%", first$year, 100 * first$share)) +
  annotate("text", x = last$year - 1, y = .035, hjust = 1, size = 3.4,
           family = dsvar_font(), colour = p$ink_2,
           label = sprintf("%d: %.0f%%", last$year, 100 * last$share)) +
  scale_y_continuous(labels = scales::percent, limits = c(0, 1), expand = c(0, 0)) +
  scale_x_continuous(breaks = c(1820, 1850, 1900, 1950, 2015), expand = expansion(add = c(3, 3))) +
  labs(title = sprintf("As a share, the fall runs the whole two centuries: from %.0f%% of the world to %.0f%%",
                       100 * first$share, 100 * last$share),
       subtitle = "Share of the world population living in extreme poverty ($1.90 a day)",
       x = NULL, y = NULL,
       caption = "The same file: people in extreme poverty divided by the two bands' total")

As a share, poverty has been falling since the first estimate; as a count, it rose for 160 years and has fallen for 35. Both are true. Which one a chart shows decides whether the industrial revolution looks like the start of the decline or a long prelude to it, and the original is the rare chart that shows both at once.

A whole-story chart is a promise to show everything that matters at once: the thing, its denominator, and enough time to see the change. This one keeps the promise about the numbers and breaks it about the evidence. The fix is cheap: draw the points, mark the seam, and the same chart tells the reader where it is sure.
