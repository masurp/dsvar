The Hertzsprung–Russell diagram

A plain scatterplot of two measurements that turned out to contain an entire theory of how stars live and die. It also runs its brightness axis backwards: the third axis-inversion case in this gallery, and the one where the inversion is least controversial.

THE ORIGINAL

Figure from “Relations between the spectra and other characteristics of the stars” by Henry Norris Russell, Nature 93, 252 (1914). Scan via Michael Richmond’s lecture notes, RIT. Public domain in the US; in copyright in the EU until the end of 2027 (Russell died in 1957). Shown at reduced size under the Dutch citation right (Auteurswet art. 15a), for the critique on this page.

“The Hertzsprung Russell Diagram” by Richard Powell, via Wikimedia Commons, licensed CC BY-SA 2.5.

Independently arrived at by Ejnar Hertzsprung (1911) and Henry Norris Russell (1913): plot a star’s colour against its intrinsic brightness and the points do not scatter. They fall into a diagonal band with distinct clumps off it. Russell’s plate, on the left, shows a few hundred stars with measured distances; the modern rendering beside it shows many thousands and names the regions Russell left unlabelled. The plate already has the essentials, including the brightness axis that runs backwards. Because spectral class was a category, the plate’s stars stand in vertical columns, one per class. The modern version uses a continuous colour index instead, so the columns melt into a cloud. And the two straight lines Russell ruled along the edges of the band are the first luminosity-class lines: the pink curves on the right, and in the recreation below, are their descendants.

How to read it

Each point is a star. Nothing else is encoded: no size, no time, no groups.

Left to right is colour, and therefore temperature: blue-hot on the left, red-cool on the right. Classic versions put temperature here and reverse it; plotting colour index instead gets the same order without reversing anything.

Up is brighter. The axis is absolute magnitude, a scale on which smaller numbers mean brighter stars, so it is plotted inverted, and the numbers count down as you go up.

The diagonal band is the main sequence: stars steadily fusing hydrogen, which is most of them, for most of their lives.

The clump above and right are giants: cool but enormous, so bright anyway. The faint hot points at lower left are white dwarfs, dead cores.

THE CRITIQUE

01 · Describe

A scatterplot of many thousands of points, with the vertical axis numbered in decreasing order. The points are not uniformly spread: a dense diagonal band runs across it, with a separate cluster up and to the right and a sparse group at lower left.

02 · Analyse

Two quantitative variables, both on position, the most accurate channel there is, and nothing else: no colour scale, no size, no annotation layer. The content is where the points are and, more importantly, where they are not.

03 · Interpret

The claim is that stars are not free to be any combination of temperature and brightness. The empty regions are the finding: they say that certain states are unstable or short-lived, which is why the diagram became a theory of stellar evolution rather than a summary of a catalogue.

04 · Judge

It holds completely. Its inverted brightness axis is not a problem, though the Florida chart’s is: the reversal here is universal in the field, inherited from a magnitude scale that predates the plot by two thousand years, and taught to every reader of the diagram. A convention the whole audience shares is not a trick; one that only the author holds is.

THE RECREATION

Show the R code 
# The original's pink lines are the luminosity classes. They are not traced
# from it: each star's spectral type carries its class (G2V is a main-sequence
# star, K0III a giant, DA a white dwarf), so every line here is the median
# brightness of the stars that say they belong to it, in bins of 0.1 in colour.
#
# Two rules decide where a line is drawn, and both are the data's, not taste:
#   - a bin needs 30 stars (5 for the white dwarfs, of which there are 153);
#   - a giant or subgiant bin must sit at least one magnitude above the main
#     sequence in the same bin. Among hot stars it does not: this catalogue's
#     A and F "giants" are as faint as its dwarfs, and a line there would only
#     draw the classification noise.
# Class II and the supergiants fail the first rule within 250 parsecs (II also
# sits on top of III), so they are left out rather than borrowed from elsewhere.
PINK <- "#ff4fd8"
binned <- stars |>
  mutate(lc = if_else(str_detect(spect, "^D"), "WD",
                      str_extract(spect, "Iab|Ia|Ib|III|II|IV|V")),
         bin = round(ci, 1)) |>
  filter(lc %in% c("V", "IV", "III", "WD")) |>
  group_by(lc, bin) |>
  summarise(absmag = median(absmag), n = n(), .groups = "drop") |>
  filter(n >= if_else(lc == "WD", 5, 30))
main_seq <- binned |> filter(lc == "V") |> select(bin, ms = absmag)
lum_bins <- binned |>
  left_join(main_seq, by = "bin") |>
  filter(lc %in% c("V", "WD") | is.na(ms) | absmag <= ms - 1) |>
  # and, for the giants and subgiants, only the longest unbroken run of bins,
  # so a class the rule above cuts into pieces is still drawn as one line
  group_by(lc) |>
  mutate(run = cumsum(c(TRUE, diff(bin) > .15))) |>
  group_by(lc, run) |> mutate(len = n()) |>
  group_by(lc) |> filter(lc %in% c("V", "WD") | len == max(len)) |>
  ungroup()

# One gentle smoothing pass, so the lines read as loci rather than staircases
lum_lines <- lum_bins |>
  group_by(lc) |>
  reframe({
    sp <- smooth.spline(bin, absmag, df = min(5, n() - 1))
    tibble(ci = seq(min(bin), max(bin), by = .02),
           absmag = predict(sp, ci)$y)
  })

# Labels as the original sets them: beside each line's cool end
lum_labels <- lum_lines |>
  group_by(lc) |>
  slice_max(ci, n = 1) |>
  ungroup() |>
  mutate(x = ci + .04, y = absmag,
         label = c(III = "III  Giants", IV = "IV  Subgiants",
                   V = "V  Main sequence", WD = "White dwarfs")[lc])

ggplot(stars, aes(ci, absmag)) +
  geom_point(aes(colour = ci), size = .28, alpha = .4) +
  geom_path(data = lum_lines, aes(group = lc), colour = PINK, linewidth = .55) +
  # ggrepel only for its halo; force = 0 keeps every label where it was put
  ggrepel::geom_text_repel(data = lum_labels,
                           aes(x, y, label = label), hjust = 0,
                           colour = PINK, bg.color = SKY, bg.r = .15,
                           force = 0, force_pull = 0, min.segment.length = Inf,
                           family = dsvar_font(), size = 3) +
  geom_point(data = named, shape = 21, colour = "white", fill = SKY,
             size = 1.8, stroke = .7) +
  ggrepel::geom_text_repel(data = named, aes(label = proper),
                           size = 2.9, family = dsvar_font(), seed = 5,
                           colour = "white", box.padding = .5,
                           segment.size = .2, segment.colour = "grey70",
                           bg.color = SKY, bg.r = .12) +
  scale_star() +
  # ONE axis is reversed, not two. Magnitude runs backwards (smaller number =
  # brighter star), so scale_y_reverse puts bright at the top. Colour index is
  # already ordered the right way: low B-V is blue and hot, so plotted normally
  # it lands on the left, which is where the convention wants it. Reversing x
  # as well — the first version of this chunk did — puts the blue stars on the
  # right and quietly breaks the diagram.
  scale_y_reverse() +
  labs(title = "Stars are not free to be any brightness at any temperature",
       subtitle = "Absolute magnitude against colour index, 67,000 stars within 250 parsecs",
       x = "← hotter (blue)    colour index B−V    cooler (red) →",
       y = "← fainter    absolute magnitude    brighter →",
       caption = sprintf("n = %s stars · HYG database v4.1, compiled from Hipparcos, Yale and Gliese catalogues · CC BY-SA 4.0",
                         format(nrow(stars), big.mark = ","))) +
  theme_sky(grid = "none")

The pink lines are the original’s luminosity classes, computed rather than traced: each is the median brightness of the stars whose spectral type names that class. Two of the original’s lines are missing. Within 250 parsecs this catalogue holds 68 supergiants, too few to draw, and its bright giants (class II) sit on top of the giants. That is the selection effect below, at work on the diagram itself.

Nothing forbids a star from sitting in the gap between the main sequence and the giants; stars just do not stay there long enough to be caught.

WHAT THE SELECTION IS DOING

The diagram above is a picture of the stars we can see, which is not the same as a picture of the universe.

Show the R code 
sel <- bind_rows(
  stars |> slice_min(dist, n = 3000) |> mutate(pick = "The 3,000 nearest stars"),
  stars |> slice_min(mag,  n = 3000) |> mutate(pick = "The 3,000 brightest in our sky")
) |> mutate(pick = factor(pick, levels = c("The 3,000 nearest stars",
                                           "The 3,000 brightest in our sky")))

ggplot(sel, aes(ci, absmag, colour = ci)) +
  geom_point(size = .5, alpha = .6) +
  facet_wrap(~pick) +
  scale_star() +
  scale_y_reverse() +
  labs(x = "← hotter (blue)    colour index B−V    cooler (red) →",
       y = "brighter ↑    absolute magnitude",
       title = "Same axes, two ways of choosing which stars to plot",
       subtitle = "Nearest is roughly a fair sample. Brightest-in-our-sky is not a sample of anything",
       caption = "n = 3,000 per panel, drawn from the same catalogue") +
  theme_sky(grid = "none")

This is the selection-effect lesson the course teaches in week 4, and stars are the cleanest case of it anywhere. Pick the brightest objects in your sky and you will conclude that the universe is full of giants, because the faint majority cannot be seen far away. The shape of your scatter is the shape of your sampling, and no amount of care with the encoding will tell you that; only knowing how the rows got into the table will.

THE SAME STARS, ANOTHER QUESTION

The diagram asks where stars can sit. Cut it into strips by spectral class instead, and ask how bright the stars of one colour are, and a different finding comes forward: at the same colour there are two kinds of star.

Show the R code 
CLASSES <- c("B", "A", "F", "G", "K", "M")
by_class <- stars |>
  mutate(cls = str_sub(spect, 1, 1)) |>
  filter(cls %in% CLASSES) |>
  mutate(cls = factor(cls, levels = rev(CLASSES))) |>
  group_by(cls) |>
  mutate(n = n(), typical_ci = median(ci)) |>
  ungroup()

# Where the two kinds of K star peak, read off the data rather than typed in:
# the densest magnitude on each side of the dip between them.
peak <- function(x) { d <- density(x); d$x[which.max(d$y)] }
k <- filter(by_class, cls == "K")$absmag
k_giant <- peak(k[k < 3.5])
k_dwarf <- peak(k[k >= 3.5])
times   <- 10^(.4 * (k_dwarf - k_giant))   # five magnitudes = 100 times

# The M row, for the caption: how far away its dwarfs and its giants are.
m <- filter(by_class, cls == "M") |> mutate(giant = absmag < 3.5)
m_dist <- m |> group_by(giant) |> summarise(d = median(dist)) |> deframe()

labels_n <- by_class |> distinct(cls, n) |>
  mutate(lab = sprintf("%s  ·  %s", cls, format(n, big.mark = ",", trim = TRUE)))

ggplot(by_class, aes(absmag, cls, fill = typical_ci)) +
  ggridges::geom_density_ridges(scale = 1.25, rel_min_height = .004,
                                colour = SKY, linewidth = .5, alpha = .95) +
  # Written inside the K row's two humps, on the fill, so they cannot collide
  # with the row above
  annotate("text", x = c(k_giant, k_dwarf), y = 2.07, vjust = 0,
           label = c("giants", "dwarfs"), colour = SKY, fontface = "bold",
           family = dsvar_font(), size = 3.1) +
  scale_star("fill") +
  # Brighter to the right, the way a reader expects "more" to go. The H-R
  # diagram puts it up; this chart has no up to spare.
  scale_x_reverse(breaks = seq(15, -5, -5)) +
  scale_y_discrete(labels = setNames(labels_n$lab, labels_n$cls),
                   expand = expansion(add = c(.2, 1.35))) +
  labs(title = sprintf("At the same colour, two kinds of star: K giants are about %s times brighter than K dwarfs",
                       format(signif(times, 1), big.mark = ",")),
       subtitle = "Absolute magnitude within each spectral class, hot at the top to cool at the bottom",
       x = "← fainter    absolute magnitude    brighter →", y = NULL,
       caption = sprintf("n = %s stars with a spectral class B to M, out of %s · each ridge is scaled to its own class · HYG database v4.1, CC BY-SA 4.0",
                         format(nrow(by_class), big.mark = ","),
                         format(nrow(stars), big.mark = ","))) +
  theme_sky(grid = "x") +
  theme(axis.text.y = element_text(hjust = 0, vjust = 0, colour = "grey85",
                                   size = rel(.95)))

This is the split Hertzsprung noticed before the diagram had a name: cool stars come either faint or very bright, with little in between, and he called them dwarfs and giants. The scatterplot shows it as two separate regions. The ridges show it as two humps in one row, which is easier to see and easier to measure. The M row splits the same way, and carries the selection effect from above: its dwarfs sit a median 22 parsecs away and its giants 198, because an M dwarf any further out is too faint to have made it into these catalogues.

The ridges give up what the diagram is for. Colour is now six categories, not a continuous axis, so the main sequence is no longer a line you can see bend. And each ridge is scaled to its own class, so a row of 1,806 M stars looks as big as a row of 20,824 F stars. Ridgelines compare shapes, not counts, which is why the count is printed on every row.
