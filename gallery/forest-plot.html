The forest plot

Thirteen trials of one vaccine, each drawn as an estimate and an interval, and a diamond underneath that says what they amount to together. It is the standard figure of evidence synthesis, so standard that it is easy to stop reading it. The diamond is the part everyone quotes, and it answers a narrower question than most readers think.

THE ORIGINAL

“Forest Plot” by Wolfgang Viechtbauer, The metafor Package plot gallery, page last modified 30 June 2026. Licensed CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. Reproduced unmodified; it keeps its own licence, not this site’s.

The figure the metafor documentation uses to show what forest() does, drawn from the BCG vaccine dataset that ships with the package (Colditz et al., 1994). Base R graphics, nine lines of code, published with that code beside it, which is what lets us check our recreation number by number.

How to read it

Each row is one trial. The four counts are the 2 × 2 table it reported: people who did (TB+) and did not (TB−) develop tuberculosis, among the vaccinated and among the controls.

The square is the trial’s risk ratio: the TB rate among the vaccinated divided by the rate among controls. Left of the dotted line at 1 means the vaccine helped. The square’s size says how much weight the trial gets in the pooled estimate.

The line is its 95% confidence interval. An arrow means the interval runs off the edge of the axis.

The axis is logarithmic. 0.25 and 4 are the same distance from 1 because halving and doubling a risk are symmetric changes.

The diamond is the random-effects summary. Its centre is the pooled risk ratio and its tips are that estimate’s 95% interval. The text beside it reports how much the trials disagree (Q, I², τ²).

THE CRITIQUE

01 · Describe

A table and a chart fused into one figure. Thirteen rows of text and counts on the left, a dot-and-interval plot in the middle on a log axis with a reference line at no effect, the same estimates printed as numbers on the right, and a summary diamond under a rule at the bottom. Alternate rows are shaded. There is no colour.

02 · Analyse

Position on a common scale for the estimate, length for the uncertainty, area for the weight. The log axis is the right call for a ratio, and the dotted line at 1 does what the diagonal does in Original against replication: it turns every interval into a yes-or-no question you can answer by eye. The printed numbers duplicate the chart on purpose, because a meta-analysis is read by people who will want to cite them. The rows are in the dataset’s order, which is the order the trials were numbered in, not an order that means anything.

03 · Interpret

The claim is that BCG roughly halves the risk of tuberculosis: a pooled risk ratio of 0.49, with an interval well clear of 1. The figure makes that claim with the diamond. Everything above the bottom rule is the evidence; the diamond is the verdict.

04 · Judge

An honest and very well made figure that hands the reader a trap. The heterogeneity statistics are printed right beside the diamond (I² of 92%, meaning almost all the spread between trials is real rather than noise), but as text, while the diamond is drawn as a shape, and a reader looks at the shape. The diamond says how precisely we know the average effect; it says nothing about what the next trial will find, and with this much disagreement those are very different intervals. Two more things the figure cannot show: that the disagreement has a cause, and that the squares exaggerate the differences in weight. Both are below.

THE RECREATION

The original is base R graphics, and forest() gets its table-plus-chart layout from one trick: it draws everything, text columns included, in the coordinate system of the log risk ratio. The author names sit at an x of −16, the counts at −9.5 to −4.5, the risk ratios printed at +6. The recreation uses the same trick in ggplot: one panel, one coordinate system, every column a geom_text() at a fixed x.

Show the R code 
alim  <- log(c(.05, 4))          # forest() sets the axis limits to range(at)
ticks <- log(c(.05, .25, 1, 4))
sw    <- sqrt(wt)

trials <- as_tibble(dat) |>
  mutate(row   = k:1,             # first trial at the top
         label = paste(author, year, sep = ", "),
         lo    = yi - qnorm(.975) * sqrt(vi),
         hi    = yi + qnorm(.975) * sqrt(vi),
         annot = sprintf("%.2f [%.2f, %.2f]", exp(yi), exp(lo), exp(hi)),
         # forest()'s default point size: square root of the weight, rescaled
         # linearly onto 0.5 to 1.5. See "How we know" below.
         weight = wt,
         psize = .5 + (sw - min(sw)) / diff(range(sw)),
         shade = row %% 2 == 1)

counts <- trials |>
  select(row, tpos, tneg, cpos, cneg) |>
  pivot_longer(-row) |>
  mutate(x = c(tpos = -9.5, tneg = -8, cpos = -6, cneg = -4.5)[name])

diamond <- tibble(x = c(res$ci.lb, res$b[1], res$ci.ub, res$b[1]),
                  y = -1 + c(0, .17, 0, -.17))

  filter(hi > alim[2]) |>

HOW WE KNOW THE SQUARES ARE THAT SIZE

The original’s code does not set the size of the squares, so forest() falls back on a default, and the documentation describes it only loosely. The rule, read out of the function’s source: take the square root of each trial’s weight, then stretch those values linearly so the smallest becomes 0.5 and the largest 1.5, and use that as the symbol size. The colours are not documented at all; sampled off the original’s pixels, the ground is #DDDDDD and the stripes #CCCCCC. Our version keeps the stripes, lighter, and drops the grey ground.

A square’s side is set by the rescaled value, so its area goes with the square of it, and the 0.5 floor means the smallest trial always gets exactly a ninth of the largest one’s ink, however close their weights actually are.

Show the R code 
ink_share <- trials |>
  mutate(area   = 100 * psize^2 / sum(psize^2),
         label  = fct_reorder(label, weight)) |>
  select(label, weight, area) |>
  pivot_longer(-label) |>
  mutate(name = factor(name, c("weight", "area"),
                       c("Share of the pooled estimate", "Share of the squares' ink")))

WHY THE WEIGHTS ARE SO EVEN TO BEGIN WITH

TPT Madras enrolled 176,782 people, 49% of everyone in all thirteen trials, and gets 10.2% of the weight. Under a random-effects model each trial’s weight is 1 / (its own variance + τ²), and when trials disagree as much as these do, τ² swamps the sampling variance and every trial’s weight heads towards equal. That is the model doing what it was asked: it treats the trials as a sample of settings, and a huge trial in one setting is still one setting. It is also the opposite of what the eye expects from “bigger study, bigger square”.

THE SAME DATA, DIFFERENT CHOICES

Two things the original leaves out, both available in the same dataset. One is the prediction interval: where the effect of a new trial, in a new setting, would be expected to fall. The other is an order that means something: the trials were run at very different distances from the equator, and the dataset records it.

The original is not wrong about anything. It reports I² and τ², it draws every interval, it is reproducible to the decimal. But it presents thirteen trials in the order they were numbered and sums them into one diamond, and the diamond invites the sentence “BCG halves the risk of TB”. The latitude plot invites a different one, “BCG works far better far from the equator”, which is closer to what the trials actually say and was the finding of the meta-analysis these data come from. One leading explanation is exposure to environmental mycobacteria in warmer climates, which gives some protection already and leaves less for the vaccine to add. Order is an argument too, and sorting by the dataset’s row number is a choice not to make one.
