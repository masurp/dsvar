Snow’s cholera map

The founding case for visualization as argument, and not, as it is usually told, the moment of discovery. Snow drew this after the outbreak was over and after he had already had the pump handle removed. It was made to convince a committee.

THE ORIGINAL

Map of the 1854 Broad Street cholera outbreak, from On the Mode of Communication of Cholera (2nd ed.) by John Snow, 1855. Public domain (author died 1858); scan via Wikimedia Commons.

John Snow’s map of the 1854 Broad Street cholera outbreak in Soho, London, published in the second edition of On the Mode of Communication of Cholera (1855).

How to read it

The base is a street plan of a few blocks of Soho. No coordinates, no grid, no legend on the plate itself.

Each short black bar is one death, stacked outward from the front of the house it happened in. A house with five bars had five deaths.

The marked points are the public water pumps. There were thirteen in the area.

The argument is a shape: the bars pile up around one pump and thin out towards the others. Nothing is calculated on the plate.

THE CRITIQUE

01 · Describe

A street plan with two kinds of mark on it: small bars clustered against buildings, and a second symbol at thirteen scattered points. Density of the bars varies sharply across a few hundred metres. There is no scale bar, no legend and no statistic.

02 · Analyse

Position carries geography, and that is the only channel doing quantitative work. Deaths are encoded by count of marks, one per person, rather than by colour or area, so the reader does the aggregating by eye and can see the individual cases that make up the cluster. A choropleth of the same data would have hidden exactly the structure the argument needs.

03 · Interpret

The claim is that cholera came from the water at one specific pump. The map does not say that in words, and it does not need to: once deaths are plotted where they happened and pumps are plotted beside them, the reader draws the conclusion and feels they thought of it themselves.

04 · Judge

The evidence supports the claim, but not for the reason the legend suggests. The map has no control for how many people lived on each street, so density of deaths partly tracks density of housing. Snow’s actual case rested on the anomalies (the brewery whose workers drank beer and lived, the widow who had Broad Street water delivered and died miles away), and those are notes in the text, not marks on the plate.

An attempt, not a facsimile: Snow drew stacked bars per address; this uses one dot per death with transparency. The cluster is the point and both encodings carry it.

DOES THE CLUSTER SURVIVE A NUMBER?

The map asks you to believe a shape. Check whether the shape holds up when you make it arithmetic, a move Snow’s critics could have made and did not.

Show the R code 
broad <- pumps |> filter(str_detect(tolower(label), "broad"))

nearest <- deaths |>
  rowwise() |>
  mutate(d = list(sqrt((x - pumps$x)^2 + (y - pumps$y)^2))) |>
  mutate(which_pump = pumps$label[which.min(d)],
         dist       = min(d)) |>
  ungroup() |>
  mutate(grp = if_else(which_pump == broad$label, "Nearest pump: Broad Street",
                       "Nearest pump: any other"))

nearest |>
  count(grp) |>
  mutate(share = scales::percent(n / sum(n), accuracy = .1)) |>
  knitr::kable(col.names = c("", "Deaths", "Share"))

STRAIGHT LINES ARE NOT HOW PEOPLE FETCH WATER

The distances above are Euclidean: they go through buildings. Nobody carried a bucket through a wall. The honest version is a walking distance along the street network, and when that is computed the Broad Street cluster gets sharper, because the pump’s catchment follows the streets. Snow himself drew a boundary line of roughly this kind on a later version of the map. Our number is the easy one, and you have to say which one you computed.

THE ANECDOTE THAT IS THE COURSE’S THESIS

The map is dated 1855 and appeared in the second edition. The pump handle came off in September 1854, and the outbreak was already subsiding when it did. Snow made this to persuade a committee of a conclusion he had reached from door-to-door interviews, water samples and a mortality table. It is a superb piece of argument and a terrible piece of discovery, and it is remembered as the opposite. When you make your own charts, notice which one you are doing.
