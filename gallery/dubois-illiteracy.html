Du Bois’s data portraits

In 1900 W. E. B. Du Bois and his students at Atlanta University, a historically Black university, drew some sixty charts of Black life in America, by hand, for the Paris Exposition. They were made to answer the racist “science” of the day with evidence. This one makes the case in ten bars: one generation after slavery, Black Americans were already less illiterate than the people of several European nations.

THE ORIGINAL

Illiteracy of the American Negroes compared with that of other nations, from A series of statistical charts illustrating the condition of the descendants of former African slaves now in residence in the United States of America. W. E. B. Du Bois and students of Atlanta University, for the Exhibit of American Negroes, Exposition Universelle, Paris, 1900. Ink and watercolour on board. Library of Congress, Daniel Murray collection (LCCN 2014645352). Public domain in the United States (published 1900); scan via Wikimedia Commons, shown at half its resolution.

The charts were made for the American section of the 1900 Exposition, in the Library of Congress’s words “to show the economic and social progress of African Americans since emancipation.” That purpose was a reply to the racist writing of the period, which dressed itself in statistics: in 1896 the American Economic Association published Frederick Hoffman’s Race Traits and Tendencies of the American Negro, which argued that Black Americans had declined since emancipation and were headed for extinction. Du Bois, a sociologist trained in statistics, took its methods apart in a review the following year. The Paris charts answered the same kind of claim with better data, drawn by Black students, in front of the world.

There were two sets of charts: one on Georgia, and this one on the United States as a whole. Every caption is in English and French, because the audience was in Paris. The pencilled “P 1124” and “[47]” on the board are later catalogue marks, not part of the chart.

How to read it

Each bar is one population, and its length is the share who could not read or write. Longer is worse.

The red bar is Black Americans. Every green bar is a nation, labelled in French: Roumanie is Romania, Servie Serbia, Russie Russia, Hongrie Hungary, Autriche Austria, Suède Sweden. Ireland is left in English.

The bars are ranked, longest at the top, so the position of the red bar is the finding: fourth of ten.

There is no scale and no number anywhere on the board, and no year. You can read only which bar is longer, and by how much relative to another.

“Negroes” is the plate’s own word, the standard term in 1900. This page says Black Americans, except when quoting the plate.

THE CRITIQUE

01 · Describe

Ten horizontal bars on a cream board, starting from a common left edge and ranked from longest at the top to a sliver at the bottom. Nine are green and carry the names of nations in French; one, fourth from the top, is red and reads “Negroes, U. S. A.” The title sits above in English and French, with “Done by Atlanta University.” No numbers, no axis, no source.

02 · Analyse

Length from a common baseline, the strongest channel after position, and the only one in use. The bars are sorted by value, so rank reads instantly. Colour is spent once, on one category, and red against green finds the eye before anything is read. With no scale, the reader can compare only ranks and ratios, never levels: the chart says less than Russia, not how many.

03 · Interpret

An argument against racism, made as a ranking. The question racist writers asked about Black Americans in 1900, wanting the answer to be no, was whether they could advance at all. Under slavery, teaching an enslaved person to read had been a crime in most of the slave states. Thirty-five years after emancipation, this chart answers: Black Americans are already less illiterate than the people of Romania, Serbia and Russia, and level with Hungary. Shown in Paris, it places them among the nations its audience counted as peers.

04 · Judge

A masterpiece, as argument and as craft: a true common baseline, bars in consistent proportion, colour spent on exactly one thing. The choice of company is not a trick: it answers the racist question of its day with the comparison that question deserved, nations rather than a hierarchy of races. Its one weakness is the missing scale; printed numbers would have made the case even harder to wave away. The gap with white Americans, which the chart does not draw, was imposed by law and by segregated schooling, and the last chart on this page shows how quickly it narrowed.

THE RECREATION

The bars below are drawn from the measured values, not traced: each length is its share of illiteracy times one fixed number of pixels per point. The colours are read off the scan.

# Colours sampled from the scan: the average of each kind of watercolour.
in_bars <- function(want_red) {
  b <- filter(bars, red == want_red)
  idx <- unlist(map(seq_len(nrow(b)), \(i) {
    rr <- b$top[i]:b$bottom[i]; cc <- b$left[i]:b$right[i]
    as.vector(outer(rr, (cc - 1) * nrow(R), `+`))
  }))
  idx <- idx[paint[idx]]
  rgb(mean(R[idx]), mean(G[idx]), mean(B[idx]))
}

red    <- in_bars(TRUE)

HOW WE KNOW

The bar lengths. A chart with no scale has to be measured. The code finds every pixel of green or red watercolour on the board, groups them into the ten bands, and measures each band from the shared left edge to where the paint stops. Nothing is typed in.

From lengths to percentages. The plate gives only ratios, so one number has to come from outside it. Black Americans’ illiteracy in the 1890 census series was 56.8 per cent, and setting the red bar to that value puts every other bar on the same scale. If Du Bois’s students worked from a slightly different figure, every value below moves by the same proportion, and the ranking does not move at all.

The same ranking, with the numbers the board leaves off. Printing them costs the chart nothing it needs and lets anyone check it, which an argument against prejudice should welcome.

The distance travelled

Literacy is learned, not inherited. A population that had been barred by law from learning to read went from four in five unable to read to one in six within a single lifetime, through schools that were segregated and starved of money. The gap with white Americans was imposed, and it narrowed from 68 points to 13 as schooling became possible. Du Bois’s ranking shows where Black Americans stood in 1900; this line shows how far they had already come, against everything put in their way. The ranking is week 2’s form, the line week 5’s.

Every comparison answers “compared to what?”, and the answer is a decision. Du Bois made his deliberately, as an argument against racism: he measured Black Americans against nations rather than against a racial hierarchy, and showed that they already stood among them. Most charts make the same choice silently, by which rows they include. Make yours openly, as he did, and know what it says.
