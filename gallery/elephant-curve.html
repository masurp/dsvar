The elephant curve

Twenty years of income growth across the whole world’s distribution, in one line: big gains in the middle, big gains at the very top, almost nothing just below the top. It became the picture of who won and who lost from globalisation. It compares positions, not people, and its own authors say so.

THE ORIGINAL

Figure 1(a): Global growth incidence curve, 1988–2008, from Global Income Distribution: From the Fall of the Berlin Wall to the Great Recession by Christoph Lakner and Branko Milanovic, World Bank Policy Research Working Paper 6719, December 2013, doi:10.1596/1813-9450-6719. © World Bank, licensed CC BY 3.0 IGO (Open Knowledge Repository). Extracted from the PDF and reproduced unaltered; it keeps its own licence, not this site’s.

Christoph Lakner and Branko Milanovic, both at the World Bank, combined household surveys from more than a hundred countries into one distribution of the world’s income for 1988, 1993, 1998, 2003 and 2008. This is the first panel of their first figure. Its shape, a body, a dip, and a trunk raised at the right, later gave it its name, and Milanovic’s 2016 book Global Inequality made it famous. The journal version of the paper, in the World Bank Economic Review, carries a no-derivatives licence; this is the working paper’s figure, which does not.

How to read it

Left to right is position in the world’s income distribution, poorest to richest. Each point is a group of the world’s population: twenty slices of 5%, except that the richest slice is split into the next 4% and the top 1%.

Up is growth: how much the average income of that slice rose between 1988 and 2008, in per cent, after inflation and in prices comparable across countries.

The dashed line is the world’s average: the mean income grew 24.34% over the twenty years, 1.1% a year. Above it, a slice gained more than the average.

A slice is a position, not a set of people. The 80th percentile of 1988 and the 80th percentile of 2008 are whoever stood there in each year.

THE CRITIQUE

01 · Describe

One line through 21 markers on a percentile axis. It starts at 15% for the poorest 5%, climbs to 76% at the P50-P55 slice, drops to 1% at P75-P80, then rises steeply to 65% for the top 1%. A dashed red line marks the average, and a three-line note explains the axes. Stata’s default look.

02 · Analyse

Position in the distribution on x, relative growth on y, and a line joining groups that are categories rather than a continuous series. Every point is a percentage change, so a slice that started with $200 a year and one that started with $39,000 share one scale. The x axis is honest about widths: the top 1% sits one percentile from the P95–P99 point. But the line drawn to it turns a group of one person in a hundred into the tallest stroke on the chart.

03 · Interpret

The reading the chart became known for: globalisation’s winners were the middle of the world’s distribution, much of it in China and the rest of Asia, and the global top 1%; the people who gained almost nothing were around the 80th percentile, which in 1988 meant mostly the lower and middle classes of rich countries and Latin America. The trunk and the dip together became a picture of two kinds of people, a global elite and a squeezed Western middle.

04 · Judge

Accurate for what it measures, and easy to read as something else. It compares the income of whoever held a position in 1988 with whoever held it in 2008, and the people changed. The paper says so plainly: “It is these compositional changes which explain the shape of the global GIC.” Chinese households moved up into the 70th to 85th percentiles; compare their 2008 incomes with the Latin American incomes that sat there in 1988 and the growth looks small, “despite the fact that the top Chinese incomes have grown substantially faster than the global average.” The authors also show that following fixed country-deciles gives a similar shape. The chart is not wrong, but the sentence people put under it, about what happened to the people at the 80th percentile, is something it cannot show.

HOW WE KNOW

Why the points are digitised. The curve is computed from the authors’ World Panel Income Distribution, incomes by decile for each country and benchmark year. The World Bank’s download links for it return “Access Denied”, and no other copy states its terms of use, so it is not in this repository. The figure is CC BY 3.0 IGO. R/digitise-elephant.R reads the 21 markers off it: the line and the markers share one colour, and shrinking that colour’s pixels by four in each direction erases the thin line and leaves the centre of each round marker. The axis ticks turn pixels into percentiles and per cent.

Checked against the paper’s own numbers. Three of the 21 slices appear as single rows of the paper’s Table 3:

And two things that do not add up. Table 3 also prints five wider groups. A group made of two slices has to have grown at a rate between those of its two slices, because its income is their sum. Three of the five pass. Two do not:

Show the R code 
pairs <- table3 |>
  filter(group %in% c("Bottom 10%", "P40-P50", "P50-P60", "P60-P70", "P80-P90")) |>
  rowwise() |>
  mutate(slices = list(filter(gic, pop_from >= from, pop_to <= to)$growth_pct),
         low = min(slices), high = max(slices),
         from_incomes = round(100 * (inc_2008 / inc_1988 - 1), 1),
         verdict = if_else(growth_printed >= low & growth_printed <= high,
                           "between its slices", "outside")) |>
  ungroup() |>
  transmute(group, growth_printed, from_incomes,
            slices = sprintf("%.1f to %.1f", low, high), verdict)

The table is consistent with itself: its printed growth rates follow from its printed incomes. The figure has the P40–P50 slices at about 66 and 67 per cent where the table has the group at 70.6, and the P60–P70 slices a little above the table. The paper does not say why. The digitising is not the cause: it reproduces the three single-slice rows above to within a fifth of a point. The shape survives either way, but the exact height of the elephant’s back depends on which of the paper’s two presentations you read.

The line in the original is honest about where the top 1% sits on the axis, but a line has no width, so the eye weighs each point equally. Bars that are as wide as the share of people they stand for put the trunk in proportion. The body of the elephant, the slices from the 10th to the 70th percentile, is six people in ten, and the trunk is one in a hundred.

In dollars instead of per cent

Show the R code 
gains <- table3 |>
  mutate(gain = inc_2008 - inc_1988,
         group = fct_reorder(group, from))
ratio <- with(gains, gain[group == "Top 1%"] / gain[group == "P50-P60"])

ggplot(gains, aes(gain, group)) +
  geom_col(fill = dsvar_accent(), width = .7) +
  geom_text(aes(label = scales::dollar(gain)), hjust = -.12, size = 3.2,
            family = dsvar_font(), colour = p$ink_2) +
  scale_x_continuous(labels = scales::dollar, expand = expansion(mult = c(0, .12))) +
  labs(title = sprintf("In dollars, the top 1%% gained %.0f times as much as the group at the peak of the elephant's back", ratio),
       subtitle = "Gain in average annual income per person, 1988 to 2008, 2005 PPP dollars",
       x = NULL, y = NULL,
       caption = "The eight groups printed in Lakner & Milanovic (2013), Table 3") +
  theme(panel.grid.major.y = element_blank(), panel.grid.major.x = element_line(colour = p$hairline))

The P50–P60 group grew by 72%, the top 1% by 65%, so in per cent they are neighbours; in dollars, one gained $568 a year and the other $25,249. The paper draws this too, as Figure 1(d), on a logarithmic axis. Which of the two is “growth” is a choice, and the elephant is the choice that makes the middle look like the winner.

A growth incidence curve answers “how did each position in the distribution change?”, and the sentence it is usually given is “how did these people fare?”. The two come apart whenever people move between positions, and over twenty years of the fastest catch-up in history, hundreds of millions did. The chart cannot warn you about that; only a sentence under it can, and in the paper there is one.
