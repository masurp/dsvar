Headlines turning negative

Twenty-three million headlines from 47 American news outlets, each scored by a language model, averaged by year: the news got more negative between 2000 and 2019. The trend holds up when you check it. The number printed on the chart, “−314%”, does not mean anything.

THE ORIGINAL

Fig 1 of Longitudinal analysis of sentiment and emotion in news media headlines using automated labelling with Transformer language models by David Rozado, Ruth Hughes and Jamin Halberstadt, PLOS ONE 17(10): e0276367, 2022. doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0276367. Licensed CC BY 4.0, reproduced unaltered.

The study scraped the headlines of 47 outlets, from Jacobin and Democracy Now to Breitbart and Newsmax, and ran each through a pretrained sentiment model. The paper’s other figures split the same series by the outlets’ political leaning and by emotion (anger, fear, sadness and so on). Everything is deposited openly: the scored headlines, the models and the analysis notebooks.

How to read it

Each headline is scored between −1 and +1: the model’s verdict, negative or positive, times how sure it is. There is no neutral class. Zero is where the model is undecided, or where verdicts cancel out.

Each year’s value is an average of averages: first over an outlet’s headlines, then over the outlets, every outlet counting the same.

The band is how far the outlets differ from each other that year (1.96 standard deviations, divided by the square root of the number of outlets), not the uncertainty of the trend.

The two annotations are a percentage change from 2000 to 2019, and a test of a straight line fitted to the twenty yearly values.

THE CRITIQUE

01 · Describe

One thick blue line over twenty years, inside a pale band, falling from about +0.08 to about −0.16 with a flat stretch in the 2000s and a steeper fall after 2013. The y axis runs from −0.2 to 0.15. Two annotations, a percentage and a t-test, sit in the corners, and the 47 outlets are listed in a footnote.

02 · Analyse

Time on x, a mean score on y, cropped to the 0.4 of a two-point scale where the line lives. The band looks like a confidence interval but measures the spread between outlets. The number of outlets inside the average grows from 18 to 47. The percentage is computed on a scale whose zero is a classifier’s decision boundary, and the t-test treats twenty consecutive years as independent observations.

03 · Interpret

The claim is that American news headlines became markedly more negative over two decades, and the annotations are there to say markedly and certainly. −314% reads as a collapse; p = 1.6 × 10⁻⁸ reads as beyond doubt.

04 · Judge

The trend is real: it survives every check below. The decoration oversells it. A percentage change needs a true zero, and this scale does not have one: score the same headlines with the authors’ other model, which starts a little below zero, and the same kind of fall becomes +2,528%. About 34 per cent of the fall comes from outlets joining the average, not from any outlet changing. Drop the percentage, say how many outlets each year contains, and the chart would be both honest and still alarming.

HOW WE KNOW

The data. The deposit includes the authors’ own per-outlet, per-year averages, the step just before the figure. Those are the numbers on this page; the 23 million scored headlines behind them are in the same deposit, at three quarters of a gigabyte per model.

The rule that is not in the code. Averaging the deposited values directly gives a line that matches the figure from 2014 onwards and misses it before: +0.102 for 2000 instead of +0.077. The difference is a sentence in the paper’s methods: an outlet-year is included only if it has at least 100 headlines. The deposit keeps the thin years, the smallest of them a single headline. Apply the rule and every value matches.

Show the R code 
all_rows |>
  summarise(`outlet-years` = n(),
            `with fewer than 100 headlines` = sum(headlines < 100),
            `smallest` = min(headlines)) |>
  knitr::kable()
outlet-years with fewer than 100 headlines smallest
679 24 1

The −314%. It is the 2019 value minus the 2000 value, divided by the 2000 value: (-0.164 − 0.077) / 0.077. The arithmetic is right. The trouble is the denominator. On a scale from −1 to +1 whose zero is where a classifier cannot decide, 0.077 is not a baseline, it is a point close to zero, and dividing by a number close to zero makes any change look enormous. The fall itself is 0.24 points, about 12 per cent of the scale’s range.

The t-test. A straight line through twenty yearly means, tested as if the years were twenty independent draws. Consecutive years of the same outlets are not independent, so the p-value is too small, by an amount the chart cannot tell you. The trend does not need it.

The outlets that enter the series later are, on average, more negative than the ones that were there from the start, so the published line falls partly because the room changed. The 18 that were there throughout still fall, by 0.16 points rather than 0.24. Both are true. Only one is labelled “47”.

On the model’s full scale the trend is a modest drift inside a wide spread. That is not the opposite of the published chart; it is the context the published chart cropped away. Which outlet you read matters more than which year you read it in.

Another model, the same headlines

Show the R code 
two <- heads |>
  summarise(`RoBERTa (the published figure)` = mean(sentiment),
            `DistilBERT SST-2 (the authors' other model)` = mean(sentiment_sst2),
            .by = year) |>
  pivot_longer(-year, names_to = "model", values_to = "mean")
pct_of <- two |> summarise(first = mean[year == 2000], last = mean[year == 2019], .by = model) |>
  mutate(pct = 100 * (last - first) / first,
         label = sprintf("%s\nwould print \"Δ=%s%%\"", model, trimws(format(round(pct), big.mark = ","))))

ggplot(two, aes(year, mean, colour = model)) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = 0, colour = p$hairline) +
  geom_line(linewidth = 1.1) +
  geom_text(data = left_join(filter(two, year == 2019), pct_of, by = "model"),
            aes(label = label), hjust = -.04, lineheight = .95,
            family = dsvar_font(), size = 3.2) +
  scale_colour_manual(values = c(p$ink_3, dsvar_accent()), guide = "none") +
  scale_x_continuous(breaks = seq(2000, 2019, 3), expand = expansion(add = c(1, 9.5))) +
  labs(title = sprintf("Two classifiers agree on the direction, and their percentages disagree by %s points",
                       format(round(abs(diff(pct_of$pct)), -2), big.mark = ",")),
       subtitle = "Mean headline sentiment across outlets, the same headlines scored twice",
       x = NULL, y = NULL,
       caption = "Rozado, Hughes & Halberstadt (2022), deposited data, CC BY 4.0.")

A smaller model starts the series a little below zero, and the same fall, divided by a small negative number, comes out as a rise of over two thousand per cent. The direction is the finding. The percentage was never a measure of it.

A percentage change needs a zero that means none. Temperatures in Celsius do not have one, sentiment scores do not have one, and an index set to 100 in a chosen year has one only by decree. On those scales report the change in the scale’s own units, and say how many cases sit behind each point, because an average over a growing room is two trends at once.
