Mayr’s radar chart

Twelve numbers drawn four ways around a circle, in a German book on statistics from 1877. The third panel is usually credited as the first radar chart. On the page before it, its inventor says when it may be used, and the rule he gives rules out most of the radar charts drawn since.

THE ORIGINAL

Figur 4, Linien-Diagramme im Kreise, from Georg Mayr, Die Gesetzmässigkeit im Gesellschaftsleben: Statistische Studien (Munich: R. Oldenbourg, 1877), p. 78. Public domain (author died 1925); scan of the University of Toronto copy via the Internet Archive, cropped to the figure.

Georg Mayr, later von Mayr, was a Bavarian statistician, and this is a popular book on what statistics could say about society. Its first part is a short course in drawing statistics: points, then lines, then areas, then solids. Figure 4 belongs to the lines. Having shown that one series can be drawn on a straight baseline in several ways, Mayr bends the baseline into a circle, and says on the facing page when that is allowed:

Zur Darstellung von Verhältnissen, welche thatsächlich einen Kreislauf darstellen, kann das in den Kreis gezeichnete Linien-Diagramm mit einiger Berechtigung angewendet werden. Solches ist beispielsweise der Fall, wenn die Sterblichkeit nach Monaten nicht für ein einzelnes Kalenderjahr, sondern nach dem Durchschnitte einer Jahresreihe darzustellen ist. In diesem Falle steht thatsächlich der Januar dem Dezember eben so nahe wie dem Februar, was nur bei der Darstellung im Kreise, nicht aber auf der Basis einer Geraden zu erreichen ist. (p. 77)

“For relations that really are a cycle, the line diagram drawn in a circle can be used with some justification. That is the case, for instance, when deaths by month are shown not for a single calendar year but as the average of a run of years. Then January really does stand as close to December as to February, which only the circle can show, and a straight baseline cannot.” (Our translation.)

How to read it

The column on the left is the data: a Zahlen-Reihe, a series of twelve numbers, with no unit and no labels. Read it as a year of months, as Mayr’s own example suggests.

Each number is one radius, starting at twelve o’clock and running clockwise. The largest, 10, reaches the rim.

In a and c the lines start at the centre; in b and d they start at the rim and run inwards. The dotted part of each radius is the empty rest of the scale.

In c and d the ends are joined by straight lines. Panel c is the radar chart as it is drawn today.

THE CRITIQUE

01 · Describe

A framed square under the title Linien-Diagramme im Kreise, “line diagrams in a circle”. A column of twelve numbers runs down the left. Four circles of equal size fill the rest, each cut by twelve radii at equal angles. In a and b the values are heavy strokes along the radii, out from the centre in a and in from the rim in b. In c and d the same strokes are reduced to points, joined into a polygon. There is no scale, and nothing on the radii says which number is which.

02 · Analyse

One variable, twelve values, encoded as length along a radius. Length is a good channel, but every radius points a different way, so no two are measured against the same baseline: the eye compares lengths at an angle. Panels b and d measure from the rim, which turns the encoding inside out: the largest value is the shortest stroke in b, and in d the polygon shrinks as the values grow. The polygons in c and d add a channel the series does not have, shape and area, and the shape depends on which values happen to be neighbours.

03 · Interpret

The figure makes no claim about the world: the series has no subject. Its claim is about method. One series can be drawn in several forms that look nothing alike, and the circle is one more of them, which Mayr allows only when the data is a cycle. Then the circle shows something a straight line cannot: that the last value is the neighbour of the first.

04 · Judge

Mayr got the rule right in 1877, and the rule did not travel with the form. His own four panels already show the risks: b and d invert the reading, and c and d draw a shape that the order of the radii decides. Panel c is now the default for comparing profiles, whose axes have no order at all, which is the case his justification excludes. The figure is better than most of its descendants, because it came with a rule for when to use it.

THE RECREATION

The data is the column of twelve numbers. Each panel is drawn by hand with trigonometry, for a reason given in HOW WE KNOW.

Show the R code 
ink <- p$ink
R <- 10   # the rim is the series' largest value, as Mayr drew it

# The engraver's lettering: a narrow grotesque for the title and the panel
# letters, an italic serif for the numbers. Use the closest faces installed.
grotesk <- c(intersect(c("DIN Condensed", "Arial Narrow"),
                       systemfonts::system_fonts()$family), dsvar_font())[1]
serif <- c(intersect(c("Hoefler Text", "Baskerville", "Georgia"),
                     systemfonts::system_fonts()$family), "serif")[1]

# The layout, measured off the scan in units of the scale (a unit is 28.6 px
# of the crop, so the circles are 20 across). Circle a is centred on the
# origin; the frame runs from -14.5 to 30 across and from -30 to 14.5 up, with
# the number column and the title band each 4.5 units deep.
centres <- tibble(panel = c("a", "b", "c", "d"),
                  from  = c("centre", "rim", "centre", "rim"),
                  cx = c(0, 20, 0, 20), cy = c(0, 0, -20, -20))
xy <- function(r, spoke, cx, cy) spoke_xy(r, spoke) |> mutate(x = x + cx, y = y + cy)

# For each radius: where its solid stroke starts and ends, and where its dotted
# rest runs. a and c measure from the centre, b and d from the rim.
radii <- cross_join(centres, mayr) |>
  mutate(tip   = if_else(from == "rim", R - value, value),
         start = if_else(from == "rim", R, 0),
         rest  = if_else(from == "rim", 0, R))
solid   <- radii |> mutate(xy(start, spoke, cx, cy) |> rename(x0 = x, y0 = y),
                           xy(tip, spoke, cx, cy))
dotted  <- radii |> mutate(xy(tip, spoke, cx, cy) |> rename(x0 = x, y0 = y),
                           xy(rest, spoke, cx, cy))
polygon <- radii |> filter(panel %in% c("c", "d")) |> mutate(xy(tip, spoke, cx, cy))
rims    <- cross_join(centres, tibble(t = seq(0, 2 * pi, length.out = 361))) |>
  mutate(x = cx + R * sin(t), y = cy + R * cos(t))

# a and b draw the values as heavy strokes; c and d keep the radii thin and put
# the weight on the polygon, as the engraver did.
heavy <- c(a = 1.5, b = 1.5, c = .35, d = .35)

# The frame's inner rules: under the title, beside the column, between panels.
rules <- tibble(x = c(-14.5, -10, 10, -10), xend = c(30, -10, 10, 30),
                y = c(10, 10, 10, -10), yend = c(10, -30, -30, -10))

recreation <- ggplot() +
  annotate("rect", xmin = -14.5, xmax = 30, ymin = -30, ymax = 14.5,
           fill = NA, colour = ink, linewidth = .5) +
  geom_segment(data = rules, aes(x, y, xend = xend, yend = yend),
               colour = ink, linewidth = .4) +
  geom_path(data = rims, aes(x, y, group = panel), colour = ink, linewidth = .35) +
  geom_segment(data = dotted, aes(x0, y0, xend = x, yend = y),
               colour = ink, linewidth = .45, linetype = "11") +
  geom_segment(data = solid, aes(x0, y0, xend = x, yend = y,
                                 linewidth = I(heavy[panel])),
               colour = ink, lineend = "round") +
  geom_polygon(data = polygon, aes(x, y, group = panel), fill = NA,
               colour = ink, linewidth = 1.05) +
  annotate("text", x = 8, y = 12.25, label = "Linien - Diagramme im Kreise :",
           family = grotesk, size = 9.6, colour = ink) +
  geom_text(data = centres, aes(cx - 9.3, cy + 8.2, label = paste0(panel, ".")),
            hjust = 0, family = grotesk, size = 9, colour = ink) +
  annotate("text", x = -12.25, y = c(8.6, 7.5, 6.4), label = c("Zah-", "len-", "Reihe."),
           family = serif, fontface = "italic", size = 4.2, colour = ink) +
  geom_text(data = mayr, aes(-12.25, 3.46 - 2.49 * (spoke - 1), label = value),
            family = serif, fontface = "italic", size = 5.4, colour = ink) +
  annotate("text", x = 8, y = -31.7, label = "Figur 4.", family = serif,
           size = 4, colour = ink) +
  coord_equal(xlim = c(-15.3, 30.9), ylim = c(-32.7, 15.2), expand = FALSE) +
  theme_void() +
  theme(plot.background = element_rect(fill = p$paper, colour = NA),
        plot.margin = margin(0, 0, 0, 0))

HOW WE KNOW

The numbers are printed in the figure, so they did not need digitising. What is not printed is which radius belongs to which number.

Where the series starts, and which way it runs. Only one stroke in panel a reaches the rim, at two o’clock, and only one number is 10, the third. So the first number sits at twelve o’clock and the series runs clockwise. That is a guess from one stroke, and the other eleven test it: below, rings are drawn over the scan at every value the series uses, and each stroke ends on or close to the ring for its number.

Show the R code 
# Panel a of the cropped scan, in its own pixels. Its frame was measured on the
# image: the panel runs from x = 151 to 723 and y = 148 to 720, so the circle
# is centred at (437, 434) with a radius of 286 px. Image rows count down.
scan <- jpeg::readJPEG(here::here("gallery", "img", "mayr-radar-original.jpg"))
cx <- 437; cy <- 434; r_px <- 286
panel_a <- scan[(cy - r_px):(cy + r_px), (cx - r_px):(cx + r_px), ]

rings <- crossing(value = sort(unique(mayr$value)),
                  t = seq(0, 2 * pi, length.out = 241)) |>
  mutate(x = value / 10 * sin(t), y = value / 10 * cos(t))
numbers <- mayr |> mutate(spoke_xy(1.07, spoke), label = paste0(spoke, ": ", value))

ggplot() +
  annotation_raster(panel_a, -1, 1, -1, 1) +
  geom_path(data = rings, aes(x, y, group = value), colour = "#c0392b",
            linewidth = .35, alpha = .7) +
  geom_text(data = numbers, aes(x, y, label = label), size = 3,
            colour = "#c0392b", family = dsvar_font()) +
  coord_equal(xlim = c(-1.15, 1.15), ylim = c(-1.15, 1.15), expand = FALSE) +
  labs(title = "Each stroke ends at or near the ring for its number",
       subtitle = "Panel a of the scan, with a ring at each value. Labels give radius: value",
       x = NULL, y = NULL) +
  theme(axis.text = element_blank(), panel.grid.major = element_blank(),
        axis.line.x = element_blank())

Rings at the values 4 to 10, with the rim at 10 and zero at the centre. The engraving is hand-made, and some strokes stop a little past their ring, but none is out by as much as a whole unit.

Why straight edges need trigonometry. Mayr joins the ends of the radii with straight lines, “die lineare Verbindung der Endpunkte”. The obvious way to draw this in ggplot, coord_polar(), does not: it draws the line between two neighbouring values as a straight line in the data, which on the circle is a spiral. The difference is small with twelve spokes and large with five, and it is why every chart on this page computes its points with sin() and cos().

Show the R code 
straight <- mayr |> mutate(spoke_xy(value, spoke)) |>
  ggplot(aes(x, y)) +
  geom_polygon(fill = NA, colour = dsvar_accent(), linewidth = 1) +
  geom_point(colour = dsvar_accent(), size = 1.8) +
  coord_equal(xlim = c(-R, R), ylim = c(-R, R)) +
  labs(title = "Straight edges, by hand", subtitle = "What Mayr drew",
       x = NULL, y = NULL) +
  theme(axis.text = element_blank(), panel.grid.major = element_blank())

# An open path through a 13th point that repeats the first: a closed polygon
# would draw its closing edge from 12 back to 1 the long way round.
curved <- bind_rows(mayr, mutate(mayr[1, ], spoke = 13)) |>
  ggplot(aes(spoke, value)) +
  geom_path(colour = dsvar_accent(), linewidth = 1) +
  geom_point(colour = dsvar_accent(), size = 1.8) +
  coord_polar() +
  scale_x_continuous(limits = c(1, 13)) +
  scale_y_continuous(limits = c(0, R)) +
  labs(title = "coord_polar()", subtitle = "Each edge bends into a curve",
       x = NULL, y = NULL) +
  theme(axis.text = element_blank(), panel.grid.major = element_blank())

straight + curved

The same twelve points. Between them, the left panel draws what Mayr drew; the right panel draws a line that is straight in the data and curved on the page. Neither line is data: the values exist only at the points.

THE SAME DATA, DIFFERENT CHOICES

On a straight baseline

Two pages earlier, Mayr’s figure 3 draws a different series on a straight base: a dotted upright at each value, and a line joining their tops, which he prefers to the uprights alone. Here are figure 4’s twelve numbers drawn the same way.

Show the R code 
mayr |>
  ggplot(aes(spoke, value)) +
  geom_segment(aes(xend = spoke, y = 0, yend = value), colour = p$ink_3,
               linewidth = .4, linetype = "11") +
  geom_line(colour = dsvar_accent(), linewidth = 1.1) +
  geom_point(colour = dsvar_accent(), size = 2.2) +
  geom_text(aes(label = value), vjust = -1, size = 3.4, colour = p$ink_2,
            family = dsvar_font()) +
  annotate("text", x = 12.6, y = 9.3, hjust = 1, size = 3.2, colour = p$ink_3,
           family = dsvar_font(), label = "…and 12 is next to 1 again") +
  scale_x_continuous(breaks = 1:12) +
  scale_y_continuous(limits = c(0, 11.2), breaks = c(0, 5, 10), expand = expansion(0)) +
  labs(title = "On one baseline every value can be compared with every other",
       subtitle = "What the line cannot show is that the series wraps around",
       x = "position in the series (a month, by Mayr's example)", y = NULL,
       caption = "n = 12. Mayr (1877), figure 4.")

Easier to read than any of the circles: each value is a height above one baseline, the most accurate comparison there is. What it gives up is the one thing Mayr wanted the circle for. The step from 7 back up to 8 at the turn of the year is split between the two ends of the axis. Figure 3 makes a further point that holds here: drawn tall and narrow the same line looks dramatic, and drawn flat it looks calm, and nothing in the data decides which.

Where the spokes have no order

A radar chart of a profile puts several different measures on the spokes, and the measures have no order of their own. Here are V-Dem’s five democracy indices for two autocracies in 2025, drawn once in the order V-Dem lists them and once with one spoke moved.

Show the R code 
pair <- c("Belarus", "Venezuela")
indices <- c("electoral", "liberal", "participatory", "deliberative", "egalitarian")
orders <- list(`In V-Dem's order` = indices,
               `One spoke moved` = c("electoral", "liberal", "deliberative",
                                     "egalitarian", "participatory"))

two <- vdem |> filter(year == max(year), country %in% pair) |>
  select(country, all_of(indices))
stopifnot(nrow(two) == 2)

drawn <- imap_dfr(orders, \(o, name) {
  two |> pivot_longer(-country, names_to = "index") |>
    mutate(spoke = match(index, o), order = name)
}) |> arrange(order, country, spoke) |>
  mutate(spoke_xy(value, spoke, n = 5))

areas <- drawn |> summarise(area = radar_area(value), .by = c(order, country)) |>
  pivot_wider(names_from = country, values_from = area) |>
  mutate(ratio = Belarus / Venezuela)
first  <- areas$ratio[areas$order == names(orders)[1]]
second <- areas$ratio[areas$order == names(orders)[2]]
stopifnot(first > 1, second < 1)   # the claim in the title, checked

top  <- .25                                    # the scale's outer ring
grid <- expand_grid(order = names(orders), ring = c(.1, .2, top), spoke = c(1:5, 1)) |>
  mutate(spoke_xy(ring, spoke, n = 5))
axes <- imap_dfr(orders, \(o, name) tibble(order = name, index = o, spoke = 1:5)) |>
  mutate(spoke_xy(top, spoke, n = 5),
         spoke_xy(top * 1.14, spoke, n = 5) |> rename(lx = x, ly = y))

ggplot(drawn, aes(x, y)) +
  geom_path(data = grid, aes(group = ring), colour = p$hairline, linewidth = .35) +
  geom_segment(data = axes, aes(0, 0, xend = x, yend = y), colour = p$hairline) +
  geom_text(data = axes, aes(lx, ly, label = index), size = 3.1, colour = p$ink_3,
            family = dsvar_font()) +
  geom_polygon(aes(fill = country, colour = country), alpha = .25, linewidth = .8) +
  facet_wrap(~ order) +
  scale_fill_manual(values = dsvar_cat[c(1, 2)], name = NULL) +
  scale_colour_manual(values = dsvar_cat[c(1, 2)], name = NULL) +
  coord_equal(clip = "off") +
  labs(title = sprintf("The same ten numbers: Belarus's polygon is %d%% larger, or Venezuela's is %d%% larger",
                       round(100 * (first - 1)), round(100 * (1 / second - 1))),
       subtitle = "V-Dem's five democracy indices. Rings at 0.1, 0.2 and 0.25 of V-Dem's 0 to 1 scale",
       x = NULL, y = NULL,
       caption = sprintf("V-Dem v16, %d. Area computed from the drawn polygons.", max(vdem$year))) +
  theme(axis.text = element_blank(), panel.grid.major = element_blank(),
        axis.line.x = element_blank(), legend.position = "top",
        strip.text = element_text(hjust = .5), panel.spacing = unit(4, "lines"))

Nothing changed but where the participatory index sits. Its value is the largest difference between the two countries, and its neighbours decide how much area that difference buys. The averages of the five indices are almost the same, 0.103 for Belarus and 0.095 for Venezuela, and the chart offers a verdict either way.

The comparison without the circle

# The title's claim, checked: the two largest gaps, and who leads on each.
gaps <- long |> pivot_wider(names_from = country) |>

stopifnot(setequal(gaps$index[1:2], c("participatory", "egalitarian")),

The comparison the radar chart was being asked for, with nothing hanging on the order of the rows. They are sorted by the larger of the two values, which is a choice too, but one that moves dots, not areas.

“Daraus ergibt sich, daß dieselbe statistische Reihe in einer Anzahl äußerlich einander mehr oder weniger unähnlicher Diagramme dargestellt werden kann.” The same statistical series can be drawn as several diagrams that look more or less unlike one another. Mayr wrote it on page 77, and it is still the argument of this course. His circle is a good diagram for a cycle, because the order of the spokes is the order of the year. For anything else the order is yours, and so is the shape.
