1 · Meet the data

Gapminder is a Swedish foundation that collects statistics on the world’s countries, from the UN, the World Bank and national statistics offices, and fills the gaps with its own estimates. Today’s dataset is a small extract of it, made for teaching: every five years from 1952 to 2007. Of the close to two hundred countries in the world, it keeps the 142 with a value for every one of those years.

The dataset is tab-separated, so it is read with read_tsv(), not read_csv().

gap <- read_tsv("data/gapminder.csv")
glimpse(gap)
Column What it is
country The country’s name
continent One of five: Africa, Americas, Asia, Europe, Oceania
year Every five years, 1952 to 2007
lifeExp Life expectancy at birth, in years: how long a baby born that year would live if that year’s death rates held for its whole life. It is not the age at which people died. Where deaths were not registered, as in many countries in the 1950s, it is an estimate
pop Population
gdpPercap GDP per person: the value of everything the country produced in a year, divided by its population, in US dollars adjusted for inflation so that years can be compared. An average: it says nothing about who gets the income

Source, for every caption: Gapminder, via Jennifer Bryan’s gapminder package (CC BY 4.0).

3 · One row per country, and a summary per group

The fix for the first lie: keep one year. Turn the dollars into thousands while you are at it, so that a bin of 5 means five thousand dollars, and keep the three columns you need.

income <- gap |>
  filter(year == 2007) |>              # one row per country
  mutate(gdp = gdpPercap / 1000) |>    # dollars into thousands of dollars
  select(country, continent, gdp)

income |> arrange(desc(gdp))

A mean is one number for a whole group. The median is the middle country: half the countries are below it, half above. group_by() and summarise() give both, once per continent, and n() says how many countries each row stands for.

income |>
  group_by(continent) |>
  summarise(mean = mean(gdp), median = median(gdp), n = n()) |>
  arrange(desc(median))

When a few rich countries pull the mean far above the middle one, the distribution is called skewed.

4 · The bin is an argument

A histogram is the data after one number you chose: the width of the bins. boundary = 0 puts the bin edges on round numbers, so a reader can say what a bar covers. The two lines mark the mean and the median.

ggplot(income, aes(gdp)) +
  geom_histogram(binwidth = 5, boundary = 0) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = mean(income$gdp), linetype = 2) +     # dashed: the mean
  geom_vline(xintercept = median(income$gdp), linetype = 3)     # dotted: the median

A density curve is a histogram with the bins made invisible. adjust sets how smooth it is. geom_rug() draws every country underneath as a tick, so you can see what the curve is made from.

ggplot(income, aes(gdp)) +
  geom_density(adjust = 1) +
  geom_rug(alpha = 0.5) +
  labs(x = "GDP per person, thousands of US dollars", y = NULL)

6 · The claim in the title

The claim goes in the title, the most-read line on the chart. The caption gets the n, the source and what the chart cannot tell you, and this week the binwidth too, because the shape depends on it. Each country counts once, whatever its population: India and Norway are one row each, so this is a distribution of countries, not of people.

theme(plot.title.position = "plot") starts the title at the left edge of the whole chart instead of the panel. theme_dsvar() already does it; try "panel" once to see the difference.

ggplot(income, aes(gdp)) +
  geom_histogram(binwidth = 5, boundary = 0) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = mean(income$gdp), linetype = 2) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = median(income$gdp), linetype = 3) +
  labs(title    = "In 2007 two countries in three were poorer than the average",
       subtitle = "GDP per person, thousands of US dollars. Dashed line: the mean. Dotted line: the median, the middle country",
       caption  = "n = 142 countries with a full record since 1952, one year · binwidth 5 thousand dollars · each country counts once, whatever its population · Gapminder",
       x = NULL, y = "countries") +
  theme(plot.title.position = "plot")

Reference sheet

Today’s functions on mpg and faithful, built into R, so they cannot be pasted into the build. The forms are in the field guide: histogram, density, box plot, violin, strip plot, raincloud.

The verbs: rows, columns, groups

Each verb takes a table and gives back a table, which is why they chain. mpg has cars from two model years, 1999 and 2008, so a summary over all of it mixes the two, as one over all of Gapminder mixes 1952 with 2007.

mpg |>
  filter(year == 2008) |>                      # keeps rows: one model year
  mutate(kpl = hwy * 0.425) |>                 # makes a column: miles per gallon into km per litre
  select(manufacturer, model, class, kpl) |>   # keeps columns, in this order
  arrange(desc(kpl))                           # sorts rows, largest first

mpg |>
  group_by(class) |>
  summarise(mean = mean(hwy), median = median(hwy), n = n())   # one row per group

count(mpg, class)        # rows per group: short for group_by() + summarise(n = n())

Always ask for n = n(): it says how many rows each mean stands for.

One continuous variable

binwidth is the argument the histogram claims nothing about and depends on entirely. boundary = 0 puts the bin edges on round numbers, so a reader can say what a bar covers.

ggplot(faithful, aes(eruptions)) +
  geom_histogram(binwidth = 0.25, boundary = 0) +    # minutes per bar: say so in the caption
  geom_rug(alpha = 0.3)                              # every observation, under the bars

geom_density(adjust = 2) doubles the default bandwidth; adjust = 0.5 halves it. The curve claims a smoothness the data may not have.

A distribution per group 
ggplot(mpg, aes(hwy, class)) +
  geom_boxplot(outliers = FALSE) +                   # five numbers per group...
  geom_jitter(height = 0.15, width = 0, alpha = 0.3) # ...and the data they summarise

Jitter only across the categorical axis (width = 0 here), never along the measured one, or the values move.

Intervals, named

mean_se is the mean ± one standard error. It is not a confidence interval and not the spread of the data: three different claims that look identical.

ggplot(mpg, aes(hwy, drv)) +
  stat_summary(fun.data = mean_se)  # a mean ± 1 standard error per group, and no data
library(ggdist)
ggplot(mpg, aes(hwy, drv)) +
  stat_halfeye()                    # thick line 66%, thin line 95% — of the data, here
labs() in full 
labs(title    = "the claim, as a sentence",
     subtitle = "what is measured, in what units",
     caption  = "n · the source · the binwidth · what the chart cannot tell you",
     x = NULL, y = "countries")    # NULL: the subtitle already said it
