Assessment · 2026

Three types of Assignments

The course has three graded assignments: a portfolio built over the course of all weeks, two written critiques, and one visualization project with your own on data. What you may use AI for, and how to say so, is on the AI use page. Every deadline is on the Weeks page and on Canvas.

Note: The final grade is the weighted average of the three. Every component is graded on a 0–10 scale, with 5.5 the passing mark. The final grade is rounded to the nearest half point (5.5 becomes 6.0). Every component must be passed with at least 5.5, and one component cannot compensate for another.

WHAT EVERY CHART HAS TO CARRY

Three things, on every chart you hand in, from the first portfolio entry to the take-home:

  1. A claim, written as a sentence. Not a description of the variables, but the thing you claiming the chart shows.
  2. Uncertainty, in some form the chart can show. This could be a confidence interval, a range, an sample size, or a concrete sentence naming what the chart cannot show.
  3. Code that runs on someone else’s machine. We ask you to submit the code that build the figure. It should be reproducible. Thus data is loading from a path that exists and the code runs without errors. If you use a dataset that is not public, you must provide it in the submission.

A chart missing any of the three is incomplete.

Assignment 1 of 3

The portfolio

One entry a week, started in the work group

Weeks 2 to 7 · six entries, each due the Sunday after its work group · individual

Each Thursday from week 2 ends with a chart and its misleading twin. You finish the pair at home as that week’s portfolio entry and hand it in on the Sunday after the work group; there is no final submission. The next Tuesday’s lecture opens with a critique of some of the entries, without names, and what it shows you goes into your next entry. Each entry is graded fail (4), pass (8) or high pass (10), and your portfolio grade is the average of the six. It counts for 25% of the final grade.

THE HONEST/DISHONEST PAIR

Every entry has two versions of the week’s chart: the one you would defend and think is honest, and one that uses only defensible-looking choices to tell a different, dishonest story. Take the second as seriously as the first: building the misleading version on purpose is the clearest evidence that you know which choice was doing the work, and it is much harder to be fooled by a chart once you have made that chart yourself.

HOW IT IS GRADED

Grade What it looks like
High pass (10) Everything a pass needs, and a twin that changes the one choice doing the work, with a sentence that names that choice precisely
Pass (8) Complete: the sketch, the chart and its twin, the sentence on which choice did the work, what the charts cannot tell you, and the AI statement. The chart carries its claim and its uncertainty, and the file renders
Fail (4) Something is missing: one of the six things, the claim or the uncertainty on the chart, or a file that does not render

Assignment 2 of 3

Two critique pieces

A published chart, taken apart

Due Wednesday 25 November and Wednesday 9 December, 23:59 · individual · PDF on Canvas

Two written analyses of a chart someone else published, using the method from the lectures: describe, analyse, interpret, and judge. This is the half of the skill the portfolio cannot test: reading a chart you did not make, and saying precisely where its claim and its evidence part ways.

You find the chart yourself, in a newspaper, a report, a research paper or on social media. It has to be one the course has not shown, and the second piece takes a different chart from the first.

WHAT GOES IN THE PDF

Each piece is one PDF with at most two A4 pages of text, handed in on Canvas. The figure and its credit line come on top of that, and so does your AI statement. It has four parts, in this order:

  1. The figure, in the file. Put the chart itself into your document, large enough to read, with a credit line under it: who made it, where and when it was published, and a link. A link alone is not enough, because published charts change and disappear.
  2. The four steps, in order. Describe what is on the page: marks, axes, ranges, and no evaluation yet. Analyse which slots of the grammar were set. Interpret the claim the chart makes, in one sentence with a verb. Judge whether the evidence supports that claim: a verdict, and a reason.
  3. What you like and what you do not. Your own view as a reader: which choices work and which do not, each with a reason that points at a specific choice in the chart.
  4. How you would improve it. Outline a better version: what you would change, and what a reader would then see that they cannot see now. You do not have to build it.

AI

AI may improve your language, not add content. Grammar, spelling and translation are fine; the description, the arguments and the verdict have to be your own. End the piece with an AI statement that says what the tool changed, or No AI used.

HOW IT IS GRADED

Each piece is scored on five criteria, each at one of four levels: Excellent (10), Good (8), Sufficient (6) or Insufficient (4). The grade for a piece is the weighted average of its five scores, and your critique grade is the average of the two pieces.

Criterion Excellent (10) Good (8) Sufficient (6) Insufficient (4)
Describe and analyse · 25% A complete and neutral account of marks, axes, scales and ranges, and every setting of the grammar named with what it does An accurate account of what is on the page, and the main settings named, with little judgement slipping in The main elements described and some settings named, but parts are missing or judgement arrives early Description skipped or inaccurate, or the piece judges before it says what is on the page
Interpret · 20% The claim in one precise sentence with a verb, including what the chart implies but does not say The claim in one clear sentence with a verb A claim is given, but it is vague or names the topic more than the argument No claim, or only a description of the variables
Judge · 25% A clear verdict argued from specific choices and from the limits of the data, including what the chart cannot show A clear verdict with a reason that points at a specific choice in the chart A verdict, but its reasons are general (“misleading”, “cluttered”) rather than tied to a choice No verdict, or a verdict without reasons
Reflect and improve · 20% Strengths and weaknesses tied to specific choices, and an improvement argued from the claim that lets a reader see something new Strengths and weaknesses tied to choices, and a concrete improvement Likes and dislikes given but general, or an improvement that stays vague Missing, or matters of taste without reasons
Form · 10% Figure legible with a full credit line, within two pages, precise and concise writing, complete AI statement All parts present and within two pages, with small lapses in the credit line or in clarity Over length, an incomplete credit line, or writing that is hard to follow in places Figure or credit line missing, or no AI statement

Assignment 3 of 3

The take-home assignment

One new piece, on data of your own choosing

Due Sunday 20 December, 23:59 · end of week 8 · individual · your own dataset

A new piece of work on a dataset you choose yourself: one met in the course, or one you bring. You present it either as a data story or as an explanatory blog post. Week 7 is where you choose the dataset and find two claims it supports. Week 8 has no sessions: the whole week is yours to write the piece.

The dataset has to support at least two defensible stories. You tell one of them, and your note says why not the other: a file with only one story in it cannot show that you chose.

FINDING DATA

Finding the data is where many projects stall. The safest start is a file the course has used, with a question the course did not ask of it. If you bring your own, choose it in week 7 and test it before you commit.

THE FIVE-MINUTE TEST

Before you commit to a dataset, answer three questions. If it fails any of them, choose another.

  1. Can you download it today, without filing an application, and hand it in with your code?
  2. Can you say in one sentence what one row is, and what each column you need means?
  3. Does it support at least two defensible stories?
Source What is there The catch
The course’s own files World Bank indicators, Gapminder and KNMI weather, in the data/ folder of earlier weeks None, as long as you ask a question the course did not. The safest start.
Our World in Data Thousands of indicators; every chart has a Download tab with its CSV Already aggregated and already charted. Do not redraw their chart, and check the original source it names.
Statistics offices CBS StatLine (cbsodataR), Eurostat (eurostat), the World Bank (WDI) Read the definitions: a change of method can look like a change in the world.
Surveys European Social Survey, Eurobarometer (via GESIS), World Values Survey Free, after registration. Read the codebook for weights and for missing codes such as 77, 88 and 99.
Political data V-Dem, ParlGov, the Manifesto Project Indices built from expert ratings or coding: say what the scale measures before you chart it.
Research repositories Harvard Dataverse, Zenodo, OSF, the replication packages of published papers Reusing data means inheriting someone else’s decisions. Read the codebook first.
TidyTuesday A clean dataset every week, with a short description, on GitHub Hundreds of people have charted each one already. Your claim has to be your own.
Kaggle Many datasets on many topics Often no clear source. Use one only if it names its original source and you can check it.

Whatever the source, the grader has to be able to run your code on your data. If its terms do not let you hand the file in, choose another, or ask the coordinator first.

TWO FORMATS

Both formats tell one story from your data, in four charts and 1,000 to 1,500 words (charts not counted), for a reader who has not taken this course. They differ in what carries the argument: the charts, or the text.

Option A · Data story

The charts carry it

  • Four charts, one question. Each chart is one step of the answer, and its title states that step as a claim.
  • A structure, chosen. A martini glass (you lead, then the reader explores), a drill-down (overview, then detail) or a slideshow (one claim per chart).
  • Describe, then explain. First what the pattern looks like, then what it goes with.
  • Text between the charts that takes the reader from one step to the next.
  • One visual language. A category keeps its colour from chart to chart.
  • The limit, last. What the data cannot tell the reader.

Option B · Explanatory blog post

The text carries it

  • A question in the first paragraph, and why a reader should care.
  • The data, introduced. Where it comes from, what one row is, what it covers and what it misses.
  • Four charts, each read for the reader. How to read it first: what the marks are and where to look. Then what it shows.
  • The choice that matters. For each chart, the setting that does the most work, and why you chose it.
  • The answer, with its uncertainty, and the other story the data could tell.

For both, week 7’s four settings are the checklist: the title, the annotation, the colour and the order. And every chart carries what every chart has to carry: a claim, its uncertainty, and code that runs.

WHAT YOU HAND IN

By Sunday 20 December, 23:59, on Canvas, two files:

  1. The piece, as a PDF: the data story or the blog post as a reader would see it. Render your .qmd to HTML and print it to PDF from the browser. At the end of the PDF come two short sections:
    • A note on your choices, at most 250 words: who the reader is and what they can be assumed to decode, why the charts come in this order, and the other defensible story in your data and why you did not tell it.
    • Your AI statement, in the same format as in the portfolio. AI is allowed as in Part II of a work group: as an assistant, a thinking partner or an instrument, never as a surrogate.
  2. The code, the data and the output, as one zip: your take-home folder, with the .qmd that builds every chart, the data it reads from data/, and the rendered HTML. Unzipped on another machine and rendered from a clean session, it has to produce the same charts.

A take-home without the PDF, the data, or code that runs is incomplete.

HOW IT IS GRADED

The take-home is scored on five criteria, each at one of four levels: Excellent (10), Good (8), Sufficient (6) or Insufficient (4). The grade is the weighted average of the five scores.

Criterion Excellent (10) Good (8) Sufficient (6) Insufficient (4)
Data and question · 15% A sharp question the data can answer, the data fully explained (source, what one row is, what it covers and misses), and its limits used in the story A clear question the data can answer, and the data explained: source, what one row is, what it covers A question and a dataset, but the fit between them or the account of the data is thin No clear question, data that cannot answer it, or data not understood
The charts · 30% Four charts in the right form, each carrying its claim and its uncertainty, with annotation and colour that make the evidence hard to miss Four charts in a suitable form, each with its claim and its uncertainty, annotated and coloured with care Four charts that work, but a form is poorly chosen, or a claim, the uncertainty or the annotation is missing in places Fewer than four charts, charts that mislead, or charts without claims or uncertainty
The story · 25% The four charts build one argument in an order that is part of it, and the chosen format is used to full effect One argument across the four charts, in an order the reader can follow, with what the chosen format asks for The charts answer one question but read as separate pieces, or the format is only partly followed No story the reader can follow, or the format and length not followed
